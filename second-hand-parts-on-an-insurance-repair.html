Can They Fit Second-Hand Parts?

Generally not, and that is firmer than most people expect. Used parts are normally only considered fair on a car old enough, classic enough or rare enough that new ones are not realistically available.

The general position

the Financial Ombudsman Service takes the view that using second-hand parts is generally not fair, with the exception of vehicles that are very old, classic, or limited edition.

The reasoning runs through the whole of motor claims: the insurer owes you the car you had, not a cheaper approximation of it. A used part of unknown history is not the same thing as the part that was on your car.

Where used parts are reasonable

  • Classics and older cars where new panels simply are not made any more.
  • Limited editions where a used original is closer to correct than a modern replacement.
  • Discontinued trim and fittings that cannot be bought new at any price.
  • Where you ask for it, because it is cheaper and you would rather have the money.

The thread through all of those is availability, not cost. "We can get a used one cheaper" is not on the list.

Betterment, the argument in the other direction

The mirror of this is betterment: where a repair leaves you better off than before. A new exhaust on a fifteen-year-old car, a new battery, new tyres with full tread where the old ones were nearly worn out.

Insurers sometimes ask you to contribute in those cases, and that can be reasonable — you are getting a new item in place of a worn one. What it is not is a general licence to fit used parts, and the two arguments get conflated more often than they should.

If you object

  1. Say so before the parts are ordered. Once they are fitted the conversation is much harder.
  2. Ask what grade is specified, in writing. Genuine, matching-quality, or recycled. The estimate will say.
  3. Ask why. If the answer is availability, that is a reason. If it is cost, say that you do not accept it.
  4. Offer to pay the difference if you want better. Often the quickest route to the part you actually want.
  5. Complain and escalate if it is refused. Through the insurer’s complaints process first.

What we fit

Your questions answered

What is a "matching quality" part?

A new part made by someone other than the original manufacturer to equivalent specification. It is not second-hand. On common cars they fit well; on some they do not, which is the argument for genuine.

They want me to contribute for betterment. Is that fair?

It can be, where you are genuinely getting a new item in place of a worn one — tyres and exhausts are the usual examples. Ask for the calculation rather than accepting a round number.

My car is twelve years old. Does that count as "very old"?

Probably not on its own. The test is closer to whether new parts are realistically available than to a specific age, and for a twelve-year-old mainstream car they usually are.

Can I ask for used parts to keep the cost down?

Yes, and on an older car paid for privately that is often a sensible choice. We will tell you where we would and would not do it — safety-related parts are a no.
