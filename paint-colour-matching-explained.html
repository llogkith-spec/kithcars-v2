How Paint Colour Matching Actually Works

Your paint code gets us close and almost never gets us right. Every car on the road is a slightly different colour from the one it left the factory, and matching the code alone is how you end up with a repair that is visibly newer than the car.

Why the code is not enough

The code tells us the formula the manufacturer used. It does not tell us what happened to your car afterwards, and three things always have.

  • Sun. Ultraviolet light fades every paint, unevenly. The roof and bonnet have had more of it than the lower doors.
  • Factory variation. The same code sprayed at two plants, or in two batches, is not identical to begin with. Manufacturers publish variant formulas for exactly this reason.
  • Previous repairs. If a panel has been painted before, it is matching somebody else’s interpretation of the code rather than the factory.

What we actually do

  1. Read the code from the car. Not from the model year. The plate is usually in the door shut, under the bonnet or in the boot.
  2. Pull the variants. Most codes have several published variants, sometimes a dozen, covering factory and batch differences.
  3. Spray out test cards. The likely variants are sprayed onto cards, lacquered and dried, because wet paint is not the colour it will be.
  4. Compare against the car, in daylight. Held against the actual panel, outside. Workshop lighting lies, and metallics lie worse than anything.
  5. Adjust if nothing matches. The mix is tinted by eye and sprayed out again.
  6. Blend anyway. Even a good match is faded into the neighbouring panels, because good is not the same as identical.

Metallics, pearls and three-stage

FinishWhat makes it hard
SolidStraightforward. Colour is colour.
MetallicAluminium flake has to lie the same way, or it reads as a different shade from an angle
PearlA tinted mid-coat over a base. Thickness changes the colour.
Three-stageBase, translucent mid, lacquer. Each coat has to be the right thickness, and there is no correcting it afterwards.

Spray technique matters as much as the mix on anything with flake in it. Two painters using the same tin can produce visibly different panels, because how the paint is laid down decides how the flake orients. That is why a metallic is blended rather than butted up to the next panel even when the mix is perfect.

There is no surcharge on our rate card for metallic, pearl or a colour change. There is more work, and we would rather do it than price it as an extra.

Where to find your paint code

It is on a plate or sticker on the car, and the usual places are the driver’s door shut, under the bonnet on the slam panel, in the boot under the carpet, or inside the fuel filler flap. It is usually three or four characters, sometimes with a colour name beside it.

If you cannot find it, we will. The registration is enough for us to look up what should be there, and then we confirm it against the car.

Your questions answered

Will the repair be a perfect match?

On a properly blended repair, yes, to the eye. What we will not claim is that the new paint is chemically identical to eleven-year-old paint, because it is not. Blending is what bridges that.

My car is white. Is that easier?

Solid white is one of the easier finishes. Pearl white, which a lot of modern cars use, is one of the hardest, because it is a three-stage finish and the mid-coat thickness changes the colour.

Can you match a faded red?

Yes, by matching what the car is now rather than what the code says it was. Reds fade more visibly than most colours, so this comes up often.

What if I do not like the match?

Tell us before you leave. We would far rather redo a panel than have you drive around unhappy with it, and that is what the guarantee is for.
