Big wheels, tight parking zones and short sidewalls

Wimbledon runs a high share of newer and premium cars, and that usually means 18, 19 or 20-inch wheels wearing low-profile tyres. Those look excellent and handle well, but a short sidewall absorbs much less of an impact than a tall one, so more of a kerb strike is transmitted into the wheel, the steering arm and the track rod. They also show uneven wear earlier, because a wide tread across a short sidewall has less flexibility to even out a toe or camber error.

Wheel Alignment: what’s involved

Wheel alignment describes the angles at which the wheels sit in relation to each other and to the road. Toe is whether a pair of wheels points inwards or outwards seen from above, the angle that most affects tyre wear. Camber is the inward or outward lean of the wheel seen from the front, and it decides how flat the tread sits under cornering load. Caster is the backward tilt of the steering axis seen from the side, which gives the steering its self-centring and straight-line stability.

On a four-wheel measurement the rear axle is read as well. The thrust angle is the direction the rear axle actually points relative to the car's centreline, and if it is out the car tracks slightly sideways with the steering wheel crooked even when front toe is perfect. Setting the front wheels to the thrust line rather than to the geometric centre is what puts the wheel straight and keeps it there. Manufacturers publish a target for each angle with a tolerance either side, and those are the figures we work to.

Signs your alignment needs checking

Steering wheel sits crooked

The car goes straight but the spokes are at an angle. That is usually front toe set on the wrong reference, or a rear thrust angle that has never been measured.

Pulling to one side

On a flat, level road with a light grip the car drifts left or right. Unequal camber or caster is a common cause, though a dragging brake or a faulty tyre can do the same and has to be ruled out.

Feathered tread blocks

Run your palm across the tread: if the blocks feel sharp in one direction and smooth in the other, the toe setting is scrubbing the tyre sideways as it rolls.

One shoulder worn away

The inner or outer edge is down to the wear bars while the rest of the tread looks healthy. That pattern points to a camber or toe error rather than pressures.

Scrubbing or squeal on slow turns

A rubbing noise from the front when manoeuvring at walking pace can mean the wheels are fighting each other through the turn.

After a kerb strike or pothole

A hard hit can shift a track rod, bend a steering arm or move a subframe. The car may still feel normal while the tyre quietly wears out.

Steering slow to straighten

Coming out of a roundabout the wheel does not want to return on its own. Reduced caster or a binding top mount will both do this.

New tyres wearing quickly

If a fresh set loses noticeable tread within a few thousand miles, the geometry is almost always the reason rather than the tyres.

When should alignment be checked?

Have the angles checked whenever the suspension or steering has been disturbed, which means after replacing shock absorbers, springs, control arms, track rod ends or a steering rack, and after any accident repair involving the front end. A solid kerb impact or a deep pothole is worth a check even if nothing feels different. Beyond that, a check once a year, or with every new set of tyres, catches slow drift caused by settling springs and ageing bushes before it costs you rubber.

  • After any steering or suspension component is replaced
  • After a kerb strike, pothole impact or front-end accident repair
  • Once a year, or roughly every 10,000–12,000 miles
  • When fitting a new set of tyres, if the old ones wore unevenly
  • After lowering springs or a coilover kit are fitted

How we align a car

  1. Road test and check-inWe drive the car to feel where it pulls and where the steering wheel sits, and we ask what you have noticed. Any recent impact or repair work is noted.
  2. Wear and pressure checkThe car goes on the ramp and we check for play in the track rod ends, ball joints, wheel bearings and bushes, and look for damaged or mismatched tyres. Anything loose is quoted first, because an angle set on a worn joint will not stay set.
  3. Set up on the alignment gearTargets are clamped to all four wheels and the car is rolled through a compensation routine so the equipment ignores any small buckle in a rim.
  4. Measure and recordWe take the readings for toe, camber, caster and thrust angle, compare them with the manufacturer's data for your registration and print the starting figures.
  5. Explain what is adjustableWe show you which angles are out and which of them your car actually allows us to move. Where a correction needs an eccentric bolt kit or a camber bolt, we quote it before fitting anything.
  6. Adjust to specificationLock nuts are released, seized threads are freed with heat and penetrant where needed, and each adjustable angle is brought into tolerance with the steering rack held centred.
  7. Centre and tightenThe steering wheel is set straight with the rack in the middle of its travel, then every lock nut and clamp is torqued and the gaiters checked for twist.
  8. Reset and retestOn cars with stability control we carry out a steering angle sensor reset, then re-measure and print the finished figures.
  9. Road test and handoverA second drive confirms the car tracks straight and the wheel sits level. You keep the before and after printout.

Parts and adjusters

Most alignments need no parts at all, only time and careful adjustment. Where a thread has corroded solid, or a gaiter tears as the track rod turns, we quote the replacement before going further. Some cars need an adjuster kit to bring camber back into range, and we use OE or OE-equivalent components from established suspension suppliers such as Febi or Lemförder rather than unbranded parts.

  • Track rod ends and inner rack ends where threads are seized or worn
  • Steering rack gaiters and clips
  • Eccentric camber bolts and adjuster kits where the design allows
  • New lock nuts and clamp bolts
  • Subframe alignment where bolts have been disturbed

If it’s left

Left alone, an out-of-tolerance angle grinds a tyre away in a few thousand miles, and the wear does not stop at the surface: once one shoulder is down, grip in the wet drops on that corner. Wheel Alignment in Wimbledon: your questions answered

I have 19-inch wheels and low-profile tyres. Does that change the alignment job?

The measurement is the same, but the stakes are higher. Short sidewalls pass more of a kerb impact into the steering components and show uneven wear sooner, and replacement tyres in those sizes are not cheap. After any firm kerb contact on a big-wheeled car, a measurement is money well spent.

Is Championships fortnight a bad time to bring the car over?

Our workshop is in Hackbridge, so it is unaffected, but getting out of the Village and Wimbledon Park is slower while the road closures and diversions are in place. Allow extra time, travel outside the 8.30am start of the closures if you can, or ask about collection and delivery instead.

My car has lane keeping assist. Does anything need recalibrating?

Possibly. Cars with stability control need the steering angle sensor reset once the wheel position changes, and vehicles with a forward-facing camera for lane keeping may need that camera recalibrated if the thrust angle has moved significantly. Give us the registration when you book and we will tell you what your car is likely to need.

What is the difference between tracking and four-wheel alignment?

Tracking measures and sets only the front toe, and assumes the rear axle is pointing exactly where the factory intended. Four-wheel alignment measures the rear as well, works out the thrust angle, and sets the front to that line. If the rear has never been checked, tracking alone can leave the steering wheel off centre.

My steering wheel vibrates at motorway speed. Will alignment cure it?

Almost certainly not. A shake that comes in at a particular speed is normally a balance problem, a buckled rim or a distorted tyre, and it is fixed on a balancer rather than an alignment rig. Alignment faults show up as pulling, crooked steering and uneven wear instead of vibration.

Can every angle be adjusted on my car?

No. Many modern cars are only designed to allow front toe adjustment, with camber and caster built into the strut and subframe. Rear toe and camber are adjustable on most multi-link rear ends but not on a simple torsion beam. Where an angle is out and cannot be moved, the cause is usually a bent or displaced component, and we will say so rather than dial the other angles around it.

Does anything need resetting afterwards?

On cars with stability control the steering angle sensor learns where straight ahead is, so it needs a reset once the wheel position changes. Vehicles with a forward-facing camera for lane keeping may also need that camera recalibrated if the thrust angle has moved significantly. We tell you before the job whether your car is likely to need it.

Will alignment fix tyres that are already worn unevenly?

It stops the wear getting worse, but rubber does not grow back. A tyre with a worn inner shoulder stays worn, and if the cords are showing or the tread is below the legal limit on part of the tyre it still has to be replaced. Correcting the angles first means the replacement wears evenly.
