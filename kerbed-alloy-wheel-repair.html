The two things that move the price are how deep the damage goes and what the wheel is finished in. A scuff through the lacquer is quick. A gouge that has taken metal out has to be filled and reprofiled before anything else happens.

Refurbish or replace

Refurbishment nearly always wins on a kerbed wheel, and for a reason that is not obvious: a refurbished wheel can be finished to match the other three, and a new one cannot be made to look older.

If you replace one wheel on a four-year-old car, you now have three weathered wheels and one bright one, and on a set that cannot be matched the usual settlement approach is the damaged wheel plus about half the cost of doing the rest. Refurbishing sides the problem completely.

Replacement is the answer where the wheel is structurally damaged — buckled, cracked, or with a chunk missing from the rim flange. Those are safety items and we will say so rather than cosmetically tidying them.

Diamond-cut wheels are different

A diamond-cut wheel has been turned on a lathe so the metal face is exposed and polished, then lacquered. You can see the fine machined grain lines in the face if you look closely.

They are repaired differently — the face has to be re-cut on a lathe rather than simply filled and painted — and that is specialist equipment rather than something every bodyshop has.

We quote diamond-cut separately rather than giving a band here, and if a wheel has reached the point where re-cutting is not the right answer we will tell you and talk through painting it instead. What we will not do is give you a number on a web page for a job that varies this much.

The 50mm rule, if the car is leased

The BVRLA Fair Wear and Tear standard allows scuffs totalling up to 50mm around the circumference of an alloy. Over that is chargeable, and so is any dent or hole in the rim, or any damage to the spokes or the hub.

Kerbed alloys are the single commonest end-of-lease charge, and refurbishment at £108 to £144 a wheel is normally well under what gets charged. If you have a handback coming, measure the scuffing before you decide.

While the wheel is off

A kerb strike hard enough to mark an alloy is usually hard enough to be worth checking the rest of that corner. Tyre sidewall damage, tracking knocked out, and in a heavy strike a bent suspension component.

We look at the tyre and the geometry on anything that has been kerbed properly rather than just making the wheel look right. A refurbished alloy on a car that now pulls to one side is half a job.

Your questions answered

Can you do it while I wait?

Not usually — the finish needs to cure properly. Most wheels are a day or two, and we can often do them in pairs so the car stays usable.

Will it match my other wheels?

That is the main argument for refurbishing rather than replacing. We finish to match what is on the car now, not to what the wheel looked like new.

My wheel is buckled, not scuffed.

That is a different job and not a cosmetic one. A buckled rim can hold air and still be unsafe. Bring it in and we will tell you honestly whether it is repairable or needs replacing.

Is it worth claiming on insurance?

Rarely for one wheel — it is well under most excesses. For all four, after vandalism or a serious kerb strike, it starts to be worth the sum.

Do you do them mobile?

No. Wheels are done here, where there is a booth and a proper cure. A finish applied on a driveway in wind is the one that peels at the lip within a year.
