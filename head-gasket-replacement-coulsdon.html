What a long climb out of the valley reveals

Driving around Coulsdon means hills, and hills mean sustained engine load. Pulling up towards Old Coulsdon or working along Chipstead Valley Road behind a cyclist in a low gear keeps the engine making heat for minutes at a time, rather than the few seconds a flat road asks for. A radiator half blocked with silt, a thermostat opening late or coolant that has lost its boiling point will cope perfectly well around town and then let the needle climb on the first proper ascent.

Then there is the opposite problem at the bottom of the hill. The Farthing Way junction with Lion Green Road is a recognised congestion point, so cars often arrive at a standstill still carrying all the heat from the climb, with no airflow to shed it. That combination of load followed by stationary heat soak is how a marginal cooling system tips over, and the gasket is usually the first thing to let go, often during a run where nothing obviously went wrong.

The chalk valleys here hold their own clue. Groundwater and surface flooding in wet winters means some local cars have been through standing water, and a cold splash onto a hot cylinder head is a known way to distort one. If your car has been driven through flood water near the Bourne catchment and has since become unreliable when hot, that is worth telling us at booking, because it changes what we look for first.

Our approach is to prove the failure with a pressure test, a combustion-gas test and leak-down figures, then quote in writing with the unknowns named. We check head flatness with a straight edge and feeler gauges rather than sending every head off for skimming, and we fix the cooling fault that caused the problem so the new gasket is not put straight back into the same conditions. Head Gasket Replacement: what’s involved

Every time a cylinder fires, the head gasket has to contain that pressure while, millimetres away, sealing coolant galleries and oil feeds passing between the block and the head. Modern engines use a multi-layer steel gasket with embossed sealing beads, clamped by bolts tightened to a set torque and then a set angle. Once that clamping load is lost, usually after the head has distorted during an overheat or after years of corrosion around a coolant port, the seal can no longer hold.

What happens next depends on which part of the gasket has gone. Combustion gas forced into the water jacket pressurises the cooling system and pushes coolant out. Coolant drawn into a cylinder burns off as white steam. Oil and coolant meeting gives the familiar emulsion. The repair is simple in principle and heavy in labour, which is why the diagnosis and the reason for the failure both matter before any spanner is picked up.

Signs of a failed head gasket

Coolant disappearing with nothing on the floor

The expansion tank level drops week by week but there is no puddle and no stain under the car. Coolant burnt in a cylinder or pushed out as vapour leaves no trace on the drive.

White steam that never clears

Vapour still pouring out once the exhaust is hot, faintly sweet and leaving a damp film on the bumper, is coolant burning in the combustion chamber. Ordinary condensation stops within a couple of minutes of starting.

Pressurised header tank or bubbling

Combustion gas entering the water jacket makes the top hose go hard unusually early and can push bubbles up through the expansion tank at idle. It also forces coolant out of the overflow after you stop.

Emulsion on the filler cap or dipstick

A creamy deposit means water is meeting oil. Short winter journeys can leave the same thing under the cap alone, so we look at the dipstick and inside the cam cover before drawing any conclusion.

Temperature climbing or swinging

The gauge creeps up in traffic and falls again on the move, and the heater blows cold at a standstill. Gas in the system creates air pockets that the water pump cannot shift.

Heavy misfire after the car has stood

Coolant seeping past the seal overnight sits on a piston crown, giving a shaking start and a cloud of steam that clears once the water has burnt off.

Oil in the coolant

Dark, sludgy or oily-looking coolant in the tank points to oil crossing into the water jacket, although a failed oil cooler or, on an automatic, a leaking gearbox cooler can do exactly the same.

Weeping along the gasket line

An external failure shows as a coolant or oil stain running down the side of the block from the joint, often with a crusty white deposit where it has dried on.

When does a head gasket need replacing?

There is no service interval for a head gasket. It is replaced when testing shows it has failed, and that usually follows something else going wrong first: a stuck thermostat, a cooling fan that never cut in, a leaking water pump, a blocked radiator, or coolant so old it has lost its corrosion protection. Any episode where the temperature needle went into the red is worth investigating even if the car seems to have recovered, because the damage can take weeks to show itself.

  • After any overheating event, even where the car drives normally afterwards
  • When a chemical combustion-gas test on the coolant reads positive
  • Unexplained coolant loss of more than an occasional top-up between services
  • Compression or leak-down figures down on one or two neighbouring cylinders
  • Renew coolant to the correct specification on schedule to slow corrosion at the gasket's coolant ports

How we replace a head gasket

  1. Prove the failureWe pressure test the cooling system, run a chemical block test that draws air from the expansion tank through a fluid which changes colour in the presence of combustion gas, and carry out compression and cylinder leak-down tests while listening at the tank, oil filler and tailpipe.
  2. Rule out the impostorsA borescope through the plug holes shows a piston crown that has been steam cleaned by coolant. We also check the oil cooler, the EGR cooler on diesels and any gearbox cooler in the radiator, because each can mix fluids in a way that imitates a gasket failure.
  3. Written quote with the unknowns namedWe quote for the gasket set, bolts, fluids and labour, and list separately what could still be found with the head off, such as machining, valve work or a cracked casting.
  4. Strip downFluids are drained, then the manifolds, injectors or plugs, cam cover and timing belt or chain come off in the manufacturer's order with the engine locked at its timing position.
  5. Remove and read the headThe bolts are released in the reverse of the tightening sequence and the head lifted clear. The old gasket shows where it let go, and the deck and bores are inspected before anything is cleaned.
  6. Measure and machineThe face is checked for distortion with a straight edge and feeler gauges. Outside the manufacturer's limit and the head goes to a machine shop for crack testing and skimming, with valve seats or stem seals attended to while it is there.
  7. RebuildThe deck is cleaned without scoring it and the bores protected. A new gasket goes on dry unless the manufacturer specifies otherwise, and new bolts are torqued and angle-tightened in sequence, along with a new timing kit where one was disturbed.
  8. Fill, bleed and fix the causeThe system is vacuum filled with the correct coolant so no air is trapped, and the thermostat, pump, fans and radiator are checked so the original overheat cannot repeat. Oil and filter are renewed because the old fill is contaminated.
  9. Heat cycles, retest and handoverWe bring the engine to full temperature, check for leaks, repeat the combustion-gas test and road test. A final check once the engine has cooled confirms the joint is holding.

If it’s left

Carrying on with a failed gasket rarely stays cheap. Coolant in the oil strips the bearing shells of protection, gas in the water jacket causes repeated overheating that can warp or crack the head, and coolant sitting on a piston overnight can lock the engine solid on a cold start and bend a connecting rod. A few hundred more miles can turn a gasket job into an engine replacement.

Head Gasket Replacement in Coulsdon: your questions answered

The car only overheats going uphill. Does that rule out the head gasket?

No, it often points straight at it. Combustion gas in the coolant creates air pockets that the water pump cannot shift, and the extra heat of a climb is when that shows. It can equally be a tired radiator or a late-opening thermostat, which is why we test rather than guess.

My car went through flood water and has not been right since. Could that have caused it?

It can. Water hitting a hot cylinder head cools one area far faster than the rest and can distort the casting, and water drawn into the intake does worse damage again. We measure the head for flatness and look at the bores, and we will tell you honestly what we find.

Is the repair worth it on a car that has done high mileage on the A23?

Sometimes it is not, and we will say so. Where leak-down figures show worn bores or the bottom end has become noisy after running hot, a used or reconditioned engine can be the cheaper answer. We give you both figures and leave the decision with you.

Will a bottle of head gasket sealer fix it?

Those products set where they meet hot gas, and on a properly failed gasket they either do nothing or block the radiator and heater matrix, adding cost to the real repair. We do not use them. The only case where one is worth considering is a car heading for scrap anyway.

Is emulsion under the oil cap always a head gasket?

No, and this catches a lot of people out. On a car doing short cold journeys, moisture condenses inside the cam cover and whips into the same creamy paste while the dipstick stays clean. A genuine failure normally shows in the coolant as well, and a block test settles the question in minutes.

Does the head always have to be skimmed?

Only when it measures outside the manufacturer's flatness limit. We measure rather than skim as routine, because every cut removes material, raises the compression ratio slightly and alters the timing geometry on belt-driven engines. Aluminium heads that have been badly overheated usually do need it.

Why did it fail, and can it happen again?

Most failures follow a cooling system fault: a thermostat stuck shut, a fan that never came on, a blocked radiator, an airlock left behind after an earlier repair, or coolant long past its life. Leave that cause in place and a new gasket goes the same way, so the whole system is checked as part of the work.

Is it worth doing on an older car?

Sometimes it is not, and we would rather say so before you spend anything. Where testing shows a cracked block, worn bores or a bottom end that has become noisy after running hot, a used or reconditioned engine can work out cheaper than the repair. We give you both figures and leave the decision with you.
