How a Bodywork Claim Works, Step by Step

Nine stages, and the two that cause nearly all the delay are the ones nobody warns you about: the engineer’s inspection and the supplementary after strip-down.

The short answer

  • Nine stages. Name your repairer on the first call.
  • The delays are authorisation and the supplementary, not the repair.
  • The engineer acts for the insurer, not for you.
  • You pay the excess at collection — nothing up front.

The whole thing, in order

  1. You notify your insurer. Promptly, whatever you intend to do. Late notification is one of the few things that genuinely damages a claim.
  2. You name your repairer. Say it on this first call, before the car gets booked into their network and then has to be moved.
  3. The car comes to us and we estimate it. On the same industry systems insurers use, so the figures are directly comparable.
  4. An engineer inspects. Sometimes in person, increasingly from photographs. They are checking the damage matches the estimate and that the method and times are reasonable.
  5. The insurer authorises. Nothing is ordered or started before this. Chasing authorisation is most of what the first week consists of.
  6. Parts ordered, car stripped. The panel comes off and we see what the photographs could not.
  7. Supplementary, if needed. If strip-down finds more, we raise it and the engineer authorises it. This is the second pause.
  8. Repair, paint, refit and check. Including testing anything electrical that came out, and recalibrating sensors where the job needs it.
  9. You pay the excess and collect. The insurer settles their share with us directly.

Where the time actually goes

StageTypicalWhat slows it
Notification to estimate1–3 daysGetting the car to us
Estimate to authorisation2–10 daysEngineer backlog, disputed times
Parts1–10 daysBack orders on newer or rarer cars
Repair and paint2–5 daysCuring time, which cannot be rushed
Supplementary, if raised+2–7 daysA second authorisation round

A straightforward bumper can be in and out inside a week. A multi-panel job with a supplementary and a part on back order can be a month, and almost none of that month is the car being worked on.

The two bits nobody explains

The engineer is not inspecting the car for your benefit. They act for the insurer, and their job includes checking the repair method and labour times are reasonable. A good estimate written in the right format gets authorised faster, which is a large part of why it matters who writes it.

Strip-down is where estimates change. No photograph shows a bent crash bar behind an intact bumper or a cracked mounting behind a straight panel. Finding more at strip-down is normal, not a sign anything went wrong — but it means a second authorisation round, and that is the pause people find frustrating because the car is already in pieces.

What you can do to speed it up

  • Report it the same day and name your repairer immediately.
  • Send photographs of everything, including the bits that look undamaged.
  • Get the authorised labour rate confirmed in writing at the start.
  • Give us the claim number and the engineer’s details so we can deal with them directly rather than through you.
  • Chase authorisation yourself if it stalls. A call from the policyholder moves things that a call from the bodyshop does not.

Whoever you claim through, the repairer is your choice. KITH is on no insurer’s panel, which means no labour time has been pre-agreed on your car’s behalf — we estimate the job as it needs doing and argue it with the engineer if we have to.

Your questions answered

Do I pay anything up front?

No. You pay the excess when you collect, and the insurer settles their share with us directly.

Can I see the estimate?

Yes, and you should. Ask us for a copy and ask the insurer for the engineer’s assessment if it differs.

What if the engineer cuts the estimate?

Then we discuss it with them. Sometimes they are right and there is a cheaper method that is still correct. Sometimes they are working to a time that does not match the car in front of us, and we say so. We do not quietly absorb it by doing less work.

Will you deal with the insurer so I do not have to?

From the estimate onward, yes. The first notification has to come from you, because the policy is yours.
