How to Tell if a Car Has Been Resprayed

Seven checks, and you can do five of them in a supermarket car park in ten minutes. A resprayed panel is not automatically a problem — what matters is what it was hiding.

The short answer

  • Sight along the car, do not look at it. Ripples show at an angle.
  • Overspray on rubbers and shuts means the panel was masked, not removed.
  • A depth gauge compares panel to panel on the same car, not to a universal number.
  • A repainted bumper is normal. A repainted rear quarter is a collision.

The five you can do with your eyes

  1. Look along the car, not at it. Crouch at one corner and sight down the flank. Ripples, waves and a change in how the light runs along the panel show up at an angle and vanish face-on.
  2. Check the colour panel to panel in daylight. Not under cover. A repainted panel that was matched indoors is often a shade out outside.
  3. Look at the shut lines and rubbers. Overspray on door seals, window rubbers, or a hard paint edge at the edge of a seal means the panel was masked rather than removed.
  4. Open everything and look at the edges. Door shuts, under the bonnet lip, inside the boot. Factory paint runs round the edge; a repaint often stops.
  5. Look for texture. Orange peel that is heavier on one panel than its neighbours, or a gloss that is slightly different, is a repainted panel.

The two that need a tool

A paint depth gauge. They are inexpensive and they settle the question. Factory paint is usually fairly consistent around a car; a panel reading noticeably thicker than the others has been painted again, and a reading several times the others usually means filler underneath.

What a gauge cannot tell you is why. A thicker reading on one wing might be a stone chip repair done well, or it might be filler over a crumpled panel. The number starts the conversation rather than finishing it.

A magnet. Crude but free. It sticks to steel and not to filler, so a spot where it will not hold on an otherwise steel panel is worth a closer look. It tells you nothing on an aluminium panel or a plastic bumper, which is most of the front and rear of a modern car.

What matters and what does not

What you foundHow worried to be
One bumper repaintedBarely. Bumpers get scuffed; it is the most repainted part on any car
One wing or door repainted, neatlyLow. Bolt-on panels, often a car park repair
A rear quarter repaintedHigher. It is welded to the shell, so the impact reached the body
Several panels down one sideHigher. That is a collision, not a scuff
Overspray on rubbers and shutsA sign of a cheap repair, whatever was underneath
Filler-thick readings on a structural areaWalk away or get it inspected properly

The checks that come before all of this

A history check costs a few pounds and tells you whether the car has an insurance write-off recorded against it. That is a more reliable signal than anything you can see, because a Category S or N marker is recorded permanently.

The MOT history on gov.uk is free and worth five minutes. Advisories about corrosion, and gaps in testing, tell their own story.

If the car is worth it, pay for an inspection. We look at paint depth panel by panel, at the structure on a ramp, and at whether the shut lines are even on both sides — and we have no interest in whether you buy it.

Your questions answered

Does a respray mean the car was crashed?

Not necessarily. The commonest reasons are a car park scuff, a kerbed bumper and vandalism. What tells you it was a collision is several panels on the same side, or a welded panel like a rear quarter.

Is a repainted car worth less?

A neatly repainted bumper, barely. A recorded write-off, considerably — and that is recorded whether or not you can see the repair.

What depth reading is normal?

It varies by manufacturer, so the useful comparison is between panels on the same car rather than against a universal number. Consistency is the signal.

The seller says it was a stone chip repair.

Plausible, and easy to check: stone chip repairs are on the leading edge of the bonnet and the front of the roof. A thick reading in the middle of a door is not a stone chip.

Can you inspect a car before I buy it?

Yes. Paint depth panel by panel, structure on a ramp, cold start, and a written report with photographs. We are not selling you the car, which is the point.
