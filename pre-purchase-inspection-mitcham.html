What stop-start Mitcham mileage does to a used car

Local driving is slow and interrupted. The Fair Green stretch of London Road is open only to buses and cycles so general traffic uses the one-way system, the Figges Marsh signals queue at peak times, and Carshalton Road pinches over the railway and tram lines by Mitcham Junction. Short journeys in traffic like that never get an engine and exhaust properly hot, which is the single most useful thing to know when the car you are looking at is a diesel that has lived locally all its life.

A diesel particulate filter needs sustained high exhaust temperatures to burn off its soot, and a car that only ever crawls to Figges Marsh and back may have spent years interrupting its regeneration cycles. So on a local diesel we read the soot loading and differential pressure values, check for stored or pending faults in the emissions modules, and look at whether the filter is original or has been replaced or interfered with. We also check oil level and condition, because repeatedly aborted regenerations dilute engine oil with fuel.

The same slow mileage flatters other things unfairly. Brake discs corrode at the edges on a car that is used gently, handbrake mechanisms seize, tyres age out before they wear out, and coolant and brake fluid quietly deteriorate on a car that has barely covered 4,000 miles a year. We measure rather than guess: pad and disc figures, tread depths and tyre date codes, coolant strength and brake fluid moisture content, with a photograph of anything significant.

It is a quick run in. London Road south to the Cricket Green, Cranmer Road to Carshalton Road, then south over the bridge by Mitcham Junction into Hackbridge, about eight minutes. Agree the inspection with the seller first, hold the car with a refundable deposit if you can, and get it here cold. The written report is yours to keep and use, whichever way you decide.

Local tip

Ask the seller directly what sort of journeys the car has done. A diesel used only for short local trips is not automatically a bad buy, but it is a reason to have the particulate filter and oil condition looked at properly before you pay.

Pre-Purchase Inspection: what’s involved

Buying a used car privately or from a small trader means trusting a description written by the person taking your money. An inspection replaces that with evidence: measurements, gauge readings, diagnostic data and a technician's judgement, gathered in a couple of hours on a ramp. The point is not to find a perfect car, because used cars are not perfect. The point is to know what you are actually buying and what it is likely to ask of you in the first year.

Most of the cost in used-car ownership sits in a short list of items: timing components, clutches and dual mass flywheels, turbochargers, diesel particulate filters, automatic gearbox condition, corrosion in structural areas, and repaired accident damage that was never done properly. Those are the things an inspection concentrates on, alongside the everyday consumables that decide whether the car sails through its next MOT or arrives at it with a bill attached.

When an inspection is worth the money

A private sale with no comeback

Buying privately means the car is sold as seen and consumer protection is thin. An hour on a ramp is cheap compared with discovering a corroded sill afterwards.

Service history that looks thin

Stamps that stop three years ago, or an invoice folder with nothing for a cambelt on an engine that is well past its interval, change what the car is worth.

Mileage that does not match condition

A worn pedal rubber, a polished gear knob and a slack driver's seat on a low-recorded car are worth looking at properly.

A car that has been repaired

Paint that does not match under a light, uneven panel gaps or fresh underseal in one area all deserve measuring before you commit.

An expensive or complex model

Turbo diesels, twin-clutch gearboxes, air suspension and self-levelling systems all carry repair bills that dwarf the cost of checking them first.

You are buying at distance

If you cannot get to the car easily, or you are buying on a deposit before travelling, an independent set of eyes is the only sensible protection.

A dealer who will not let you road test properly

Any reluctance to let the car be driven cold, or inspected by a third party, tells you something before we even start.

You simply want a second opinion

Plenty of people book an inspection on a car they already like, purely to check there is nothing they have missed.

What we inspect

  1. Identity and paperworkWe compare the VIN on the chassis and the plate with the V5C, check the registration against the free gov.uk MOT history, and read the mileage pattern across past tests for anything that does not follow.
  2. Cold start and idleThe first start tells you about rattles from timing chains, tappet noise, a smoky diesel and a weak battery. We listen before the engine warms and hides things.
  3. Body and paint surveyPanel gaps, shut lines and fixings are checked, and a paint depth gauge is run over each panel. Readings far above factory thickness show filler or a respray, and we record where.
  4. On the rampUnderneath we look at sills, jacking points, floor pans, subframe mountings and suspension turrets for corrosion or distortion, and check for oil, coolant, gearbox and power steering leaks.
  5. Running gear measurementBrake pad thickness and disc lipping are measured, tread depth is taken on all wheels including the spare, and tyre date codes and brands are recorded. Joints and bushes are levered for play.
  6. Full diagnostic scanEvery module is scanned for stored and pending codes, and we check whether the emissions readiness monitors have completed. Monitors that are not ready suggest the codes were cleared very recently.
  7. Fluids and service itemsOil condition and level, coolant strength, brake fluid moisture content and the state of the air and pollen filters give a fair picture of how the car has been looked after.
  8. Road testWe drive the car to assess clutch bite and flywheel rattle, gear selection, kickdown, brake judder, steering feel, noise from bearings and whether it holds a straight line.
  9. Report and conversationYou get a written report grouping findings into faults now, attention soon and points to note, with photographs of anything significant. We then talk it through so you can decide what to do.

Pre-Purchase Inspection in Mitcham: your questions answered

The diesel I am looking at has only done short local journeys. What should worry me?

Mainly the particulate filter, plus oil condition and the state of the brakes. Short trips interrupt the regeneration the filter needs, and fuel can dilute the engine oil in the process. None of that is automatically fatal, but it is worth measuring before you buy rather than discovering at the first warning light.

Can I wait at the workshop while you inspect it?

You are welcome to, though it is two to three hours and we would rather you were not watching over our shoulder than relaxed. Most people drop the car and come back for the conversation at the end, which is the part that matters. Being eight minutes away makes either option easy.

Is the report mine even if I decide not to buy the car?

Yes. You are paying for the inspection, so the report and the photographs are yours regardless of the outcome, and we have no obligation to the seller at all. Plenty of our reports end with a buyer walking away, and that is the inspection doing its job.

What can an inspection not tell me?

It cannot see inside a sealed engine or gearbox without dismantling, so internal bearing wear, a marginal head gasket or a gearbox that only misbehaves when hot may not show up. It cannot predict when a healthy component will fail, and it cannot prove that a well-repaired accident car was repaired badly. It is an informed snapshot of one day, not a warranty.

Do you check whether there is finance on the car?

No, and no physical inspection can. Outstanding finance, insurance write-off markers and stolen records live in data held by finance houses and insurers, so you need a separate provenance check from a data provider. We always recommend running one alongside the inspection, because a mechanically excellent car with finance against it is still not safely yours.

Can you tell me how much life is left in the clutch?

Only approximately. We can judge bite point height, whether it slips under load, and listen for dual mass flywheel rattle at idle and on release, which together give a fair indication. Putting a percentage on remaining friction material is guesswork unless the gearbox comes off, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Will the report give me something to negotiate with?

Usually, yes. Measured figures carry far more weight in a conversation than an impression, so a report showing 3mm of pad left, two tyres near the limit and an overdue cambelt is a concrete basis for a price discussion. It is equally useful when it comes back clean and simply confirms the car is what the seller said.

Does an inspection replace an MOT?

No. The two look at different things. An MOT is a legal pass or fail against a defined standard on the day, while an inspection is broader and judges condition, wear and cost to come, including items an MOT never covers such as engine internals, gearbox behaviour and service history. Many buyers want both.
