Hill starts, wet lanes and front tyres that wear first

Coulsdon sits on the North Downs, and many of its roads are steep. On front-wheel-drive cars, pulling away uphill towards Old Coulsdon, braking down the other side and turning on winding lanes all load the front tyres far more than the rear. It is common to see the fronts on local cars wear out well before the rears, sometimes by a wide margin. Checking tread depth on each axle separately, and rotating where the car allows, helps a set last longer.

Wet grip matters more here than in flatter areas. The rural lanes around Coulsdon Common and Chipstead Valley are narrow and winding, often damp under trees and shared with walkers, cyclists and horse riders. A tyre at the legal 1.6mm limit stops noticeably further in the wet than a new one, which is why many drivers choose to replace at around 3mm. When we measure your tread, we tell you honestly where you stand rather than pushing tyres you do not yet need.

Busy A-roads add their own wear. The A23 Farthing Way junction with Lion Green Road queues at peak times, and commuters driving to Coulsdon South or Coulsdon Town stations do a lot of short, stop-start trips. When we fit new tyres, we check each rim for damage and corrosion on the bead seat, fit new valves or TPMS service kits, balance every wheel and torque it to the manufacturer's figure. If the old tyres show edge wear, we recommend an alignment check, because hills explain faster wear but not uneven wear.

Tyre Fitting: what’s involved

Your tyres are the only part of the car in contact with the road, so their condition directly affects braking, steering and grip in the wet. The legal minimum tread depth in the UK is 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tread, all the way round. Wet-weather grip falls off noticeably well before that, so many drivers change tyres at around 3mm.

The size code on the sidewall, such as 205/55 R16 91V, tells you the width in millimetres, the sidewall height as a percentage of width, radial construction, the rim diameter in inches, the load index and the speed rating. A four-digit date code shows the week and year of manufacture. Getting the right size, load and speed rating matters for safety and for your insurance.

Signs you need new tyres

Tread close to the limit

Tread wear indicators are small raised bars in the main grooves. When the tread is level with them, the tyre is at or near 1.6mm and needs replacing.

Uneven wear

Wear on one edge usually points to wheel alignment problems, while wear on both edges or down the centre suggests the tyre has been run under- or over-inflated.

Cuts, bulges or cracks

A bulge in the sidewall means internal damage, often from a pothole or kerb, and the tyre can fail without warning. Deep cuts and cracking also mean replacement.

Tyre pressure warning light

A TPMS light means one or more tyres has lost pressure or a sensor has a fault. It should be checked promptly, and a lit TPMS light is an MOT fail on cars first used from 2012.

Slow puncture

A tyre that needs air every week or two may have a nail, a corroded rim seal or a leaking valve. Many tread punctures can be repaired, sidewall damage can't.

Vibration at speed

A shake through the steering wheel at motorway speeds often means a wheel is out of balance, has lost a weight, or a tyre has internal damage.

Old tyres

Rubber hardens with age even if the tread looks good. Tyres showing cracking or that are many years old are worth inspecting, particularly on low-mileage cars.

When should tyres be replaced?

Replace tyres before they reach 1.6mm, and ideally around 3mm if you want to keep good wet grip. Any tyre with a bulge, a deep cut, exposed cords or sidewall damage should be replaced straight away, whatever the tread depth. Check tread and pressures at least once a month and before long journeys. Where possible, replace tyres in pairs on the same axle so grip and handling stay balanced side to side.

  • Legal minimum: 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tread, all the way round
  • Recommended replacement: around 3mm for better wet grip
  • Immediately for bulges, cuts, exposed cords or sidewall damage
  • Check pressures at least monthly and before long trips
  • Replace in pairs on the same axle where possible

How we fit tyres

  1. Check-in and tyre checkWe check the size, load index and speed rating your car needs, the condition of the other tyres, and whether it has run-flats or TPMS sensors.
  2. Remove the wheelThe wheel comes off the car, including locking wheel nuts using your key, and the old tyre is deflated.
  3. Demount the old tyreThe tyre is removed on a tyre machine, taking care around TPMS sensors and with alloy wheels, so rims aren't marked.
  4. Inspect the rim and valveWe check the rim for cracks, buckles and corrosion on the bead seat, and fit a new valve, or a TPMS service kit where sensors are fitted.
  5. Fit and inflateThe new tyre is mounted in the correct direction or orientation for directional and asymmetric tyres, seated on the bead and inflated.
  6. BalanceEach wheel is balanced on a wheel balancer and weights are fitted to remove vibration at speed.
  7. Refit and torqueThe wheel is refitted and the nuts or bolts tightened with a torque wrench to the manufacturer's figure, then pressures are set to the placard.
  8. TPMS and handoverWe reset or relearn the TPMS where required and check for warnings. If the old tyres showed alignment wear, we tell you so it can be checked.

About Coulsdon

Coulsdon is a town on the chalk hills of the North Downs at the southern edge of Greater London, with Old Coulsdon on the hill to the east being London's southernmost settlement. The town centre grew up in the valley of Smitham Bottom after the railway arrived, and the surrounding streets are mainly inter-war and post-war semis and detached houses, with newer homes on the former Cane Hill Hospital site. Questions

Tyre Fitting in Coulsdon: your questions answered

Why do my front tyres wear so much faster than the rears?

On front-wheel-drive cars the fronts do the steering, most of the braking and all of the driving, and Coulsdon's hills add to that load. Rotating tyres front to rear where your car allows, and checking alignment, helps even out the wear.

Should I replace my tyres before they reach 1.6mm?

The legal limit is 1.6mm, but wet grip drops off well before then, so many drivers change at around 3mm. On damp, winding lanes like those around Coulsdon Common, that extra margin is worth having.

Can you recover my car from Old Coulsdon with a damaged tyre?

Yes, we can arrange recovery or collection if the car isn't safe to drive. Once the car is with us, we inspect the wheel as well as the tyre, since a hard knock on a kerb or lane edge can damage both.

What does the code on my tyre sidewall mean?

Take 205/55 R16 91V as an example: 205 is the width in millimetres, 55 is the sidewall height as a percentage of the width, R means radial, 16 is the rim size in inches, 91 is the load index and V is the speed rating. The four-digit DOT code, such as 2324, shows the week and year the tyre was made.

Can I fit different tyres front and rear?

Yes, as long as both tyres on the same axle match in size and type. It is illegal to mix radial and cross-ply tyres on the same axle. Some four-wheel drive cars need all four tyres to be closely matched in size and wear, so check before fitting a single tyre.

What is TPMS?

A tyre pressure monitoring system warns you when a tyre loses pressure. Direct systems use a sensor inside each wheel, usually part of the valve, while indirect systems use the ABS sensors to spot a tyre turning at a different rate. All new cars sold in the UK since November 2014 have one.

Do I need a wheel alignment with new tyres?

Not always. If the old tyres wore evenly and the car drives straight, alignment is probably fine. If they wore on one edge, or the car pulls or the steering wheel is off-centre, the alignment should be checked, or the new tyres will wear the same way.

Can a puncture be repaired?

Often, if the damage is in the central part of the tread and within size limits set by the British Standard for tyre repairs. Damage to the shoulder or sidewall, or a tyre that has been driven on flat, can't be safely repaired. Some run-flat manufacturers don't approve repairs at all.
