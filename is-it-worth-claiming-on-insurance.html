Claim, or Pay for It Yourself?

It is arithmetic, and most people only count one of the four numbers. The excess is the obvious one. The lost no-claims discount is usually the bigger one.

The short answer

  • Four numbers, not one: repair, excess, lost discount, five years of premiums.
  • Non-fault with a traced third party — claim.
  • Nobody to recover from — it is a fault claim, so do the sum.
  • Notify either way. Notifying is not claiming.

The four numbers

Where to get it
1. The repair costA real estimate — the box below, or bring the car in
2. Your excessYour policy schedule. Add compulsory and voluntary together, because both apply
3. What losing your no-claims costsAsk your insurer what your renewal becomes with the claim on it
4. Five years of slightly higher premiumsClaims are declarable for five years, not one

Numbers three and four are the ones people skip, and together they are frequently larger than the excess. An insurer will tell you number three if you ask directly enough: “what would my renewal be with this claim on it?”

Fault and non-fault change everything

If there is an identified third party at fault, your insurer recovers its money from theirs, your no-claims is restored and your excess usually comes back. Numbers three and four largely drop out, and claiming is normally the right answer.

If there is nobody to recover from — vandalism, a hit and run, weather, your own mistake, a car park shunt where the driver was never found — it is recorded as a fault claim, your discount takes the hit, and all four numbers count.

Worked through

Say a scuffed door and wing, your excess is £350, and your insurer tells you the claim puts about £180 a year on your renewal.

  1. Repair cost, priced properly. Not a guess. A real figure for the actual damage.
  2. Add the excess to what claiming costs over time. £350 plus roughly £180 a year while the claim is declarable. That is well over £1,000 of real cost before the insurer pays anything.
  3. Compare. If the repair comes in under that, paying is cheaper and your record stays clean.
  4. Remember the excess is not a discount. Claiming on a £500 repair with a £350 excess buys you £150 of help and costs you years of discount.

That last point is where most of the bad decisions live. People claim on jobs barely above their excess because the claim feels free.

Claim regardless of the sum when

  • Anyone was injured, however slightly. Injury claims have long tails and you do not want to have handled it privately.
  • There is any question about structure or safety. A door that will not shut properly, a suspicious panel gap, anything that was a real impact rather than a scrape.
  • A third party is involved at all. Even if you intend to pay, tell your insurer. Settling privately and then having the other party claim anyway is a genuinely bad position.
  • The repair is a large fraction of the car’s value. That is what insurance is for.

Tell your insurer either way

Almost every policy requires you to notify an incident whether or not you claim. Notifying is not claiming, and saying so explicitly on the call — “notification only, I am not claiming” — is worth doing.

Not mentioning an incident is how people find an unrelated claim refused later for non-disclosure. The notification costs you nothing; the omission can cost you a lot.

Your questions answered

Does my excess get refunded on a non-fault claim?

Usually, once your insurer recovers from the other side. Ask how long that takes, because it can be months.

Will one claim really raise my premium for five years?

Claims are normally declarable for five years and most insurers ask about three. The effect fades but it is not one renewal.

What if I claim and then withdraw?

A notified-and-withdrawn incident can still appear on your record. Ask before you start the claim rather than after.

Is protected no-claims the answer?

It protects the discount, not the premium — your underlying rate can still rise. It helps, and it is not a free pass.

Can you tell me which is cheaper?

We can give you the repair figure, which is the number you are missing. The other three come from your insurer, and asking them directly is the only way to get them right.
