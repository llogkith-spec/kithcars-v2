Aluminium Panels Are Not Thin Steel

Aluminium does not dent like steel, does not spring back like steel, and corrodes if it so much as meets steel dust. It is a genuinely different repair, and knowing whether your car has it matters before anyone quotes.

The short answer

Which cars have it

Aluminium turns up three ways: a full aluminium body, selected panels on an otherwise steel car, or mixed structure.

Typical examples
Largely aluminium bodiesJaguar XE, XF and F-Pace; Range Rover and Range Rover Sport; Audi A8; Tesla Model S
Aluminium bonnets or wings on steel carsCommon across premium marques from the 2010s, and on some mainstream models for weight saving
Mixed structureIncreasingly normal — aluminium outer panels over a steel or high-strength steel shell

The middle row is the one that catches people. A car can have an aluminium bonnet and steel everything else, so the same dent is two different jobs depending on which panel it landed on. A magnet settles it in two seconds.

Why the repair differs

  • No memory. Steel has a degree of spring to it and wants to return toward its original shape. Aluminium does not. It stays where it is pushed, which changes how a dent is worked out.
  • It work-hardens. Every time aluminium is moved it gets harder and more brittle in that spot. Work it too much and it cracks rather than straightens, and a crack in a panel is not recoverable.
  • Heat is unforgiving. Aluminium loses strength at lower temperatures than steel and gives you almost no visual warning, because it does not glow through colours as it heats. Too much heat and the panel is weaker than it looks.
  • It is joined differently. A great deal of aluminium structure is riveted and bonded rather than spot welded, so a structural repair follows the manufacturer’s procedure for rivet type and adhesive rather than a welder setting.

Steel dust is the real hazard

Put steel filings on aluminium, add moisture, and you get galvanic corrosion: the two metals in contact with an electrolyte form a cell and the aluminium corrodes. It starts as pinprick blisters under the paint and it does not stop.

This is why aluminium work is kept away from steel work — separate area, separate tools, separate extraction. Not fussiness. A shop that grinds steel in the same bay with the same kit is seeding corrosion into the panel it just repaired, and it will not show up for a year.

If your car has aluminium panels, ask any bodyshop how they keep steel and aluminium apart. It is a fair question and the answer tells you a lot.

What it means for cost and time

Expect both to be higher than the equivalent steel repair, for reasons that are structural rather than opportunistic: the work is slower, the margin for error is smaller, the consumables differ, and more jobs end at replacement rather than repair because a work-hardened or cracked panel cannot be saved.

The honest version is that an aluminium wing with a sharp crease is more often a replacement than the same damage in steel. That is not a sales position, it is what the metal does.

Your questions answered

How do I know if my panel is aluminium?

A fridge magnet. It will not stick to aluminium. Try the bonnet and a door separately on the same car, because they are often different.

Can an aluminium dent be pushed out?

Sometimes, carefully, and with the knowledge that the metal gets harder each time it moves. It is not the repeated gentle persuasion that works on steel.

Why is my quote higher than my friend’s for the same dent?

If one car has an aluminium panel and the other does not, that is why. Same damage, different metal, different job.

Is aluminium weaker?

No — it is used because it is light and strong. It is less forgiving to repair, which is a different thing from being weak.

Does it rust?

Not like steel, but it corrodes, particularly where it has met steel or had steel dust on it. The result looks like blistering under the paint rather than brown bubbling.
