Brake Repairs: what’s involved

When you press the pedal, the master cylinder pushes brake fluid down the pipes and flexible hoses to the callipers, which clamp the pads against the discs. The friction turns your speed into heat. Pads and discs wear with every stop, so they are the parts most often replaced, but callipers seize, slide pins corrode, hoses perish and brake fluid absorbs moisture over time.

Most brake problems give plenty of warning: a squeal from the pad wear indicator, a vibration through the pedal, a pull to one side or a pedal that feels soft. Dealing with them early usually keeps the job to pads and discs. Left alone, a worn pad can score the disc down to the metal backing plate and a sticking calliper can overheat the whole corner, which costs more and makes the car less safe to stop.

Signs your brakes need attention

Squealing when braking

Many pads have a small metal tab that squeals against the disc once the friction material is nearly worn. Some cars use an electronic wear sensor that lights a warning on the dash instead.

Grinding or scraping

A harsh grinding usually means the pad material has gone and the steel backing plate is contacting the disc. Stop driving as soon as it is safe; the disc will almost certainly need replacing.

Vibration through the pedal or steering

A pulsing pedal or shaking wheel under braking is usually uneven disc thickness, often described as warped discs. It tends to be worst when braking from higher speeds.

Car pulls to one side

A pull under braking points to a seized calliper piston, sticking slide pins or a collapsed flexible hose on one side. It can also be contaminated pads on one wheel.

Soft, spongy or long pedal

Air in the system, old fluid that has absorbed moisture, a leak or a failing master cylinder can all make the pedal travel further than normal. This needs checking straight away.

Hot wheel or burning smell

If one wheel is much hotter than the others after a drive, or you smell hot brakes, a calliper or handbrake mechanism may be dragging.

Brake warning light

The red brake light can mean low fluid level, which usually follows pad wear or a leak. An amber light may be a pad wear sensor, ABS or electronic parking brake fault.

Handbrake not holding

Excessive lever travel or a car that rolls on a slope suggests stretched cables, seized rear callipers or worn rear shoes or pads.

When should brakes be replaced?

There is no fixed mileage for pads and discs because wear depends on how and where you drive. Stop-start town traffic wears brakes far faster than motorway miles, and heavier cars and SUVs get through them quicker. We replace pads when they reach the manufacturer's minimum thickness and discs when they are below the minimum thickness stamped on the disc, or are badly scored, lipped or corroded. Brake fluid is different: it is replaced on a time basis because it absorbs moisture even if the car is rarely used.

  • Front pads: commonly 20,000–40,000 miles, less in heavy town use
  • Discs: often every second set of pads, or sooner if scored or below minimum thickness
  • Brake fluid: most manufacturers specify every 2 years, regardless of mileage
  • Rear brakes usually last longer than the fronts, but rear discs on low-mileage cars often need replacing because of corrosion
  • Any pull, vibration, noise or change in pedal feel should be checked promptly

Brake Repairs in Norbury: your questions answered

My brake pads have worn unevenly on one side. Why?

Usually a sticking calliper slide pin or a partly seized calliper piston, so the pad on that side either stays in contact with the disc or does most of the work. We clean and lubricate the slide pins with the correct grease, or replace the calliper if it has seized.

Do you check brake hoses and pipes as well as pads?

Yes. We inspect the flexible hoses for cracks and bulges and the metal pipes for corrosion, as both matter for safety and the MOT. If a pipe or hose needs replacing, it goes on the written quote with the reason.

Why is my brake pedal lower than it used to be?

A pedal that travels further can mean worn pads, air in the system, old fluid, a leak or a failing master cylinder. As the pads wear, the fluid level in the reservoir drops, which is a useful clue. Have it checked soon rather than topping up and carrying on.

Do I always need new discs when I replace the pads?

No. If the discs are above minimum thickness, not badly scored or lipped and run true, new pads alone are fine. We measure the discs and tell you honestly. Many cars need discs every second pad change.

Why do my new brakes feel different?

New pads need to bed in against the discs. For the first 100–200 miles, brake progressively and avoid heavy stops where you can. Braking will improve as the surfaces match up.

How often should brake fluid be changed?

Most manufacturers specify every two years. Brake fluid absorbs moisture from the air, which lowers its boiling point and can cause a soft pedal under heavy use. We can test the moisture content to show you where yours is.

My rear discs are rusty but the car has low mileage. Why?

Rear brakes do less work, so they do not get hot enough or used hard enough to keep the disc surface clean. Cars that sit a lot or do short trips often develop corrosion on the rear discs that affects braking and can fail an MOT.

Can you work on cars with an electronic handbrake?

Yes. Electronic parking brakes need to be put into service mode with diagnostic equipment before rear pads are changed, then recalibrated afterwards. We do this as part of the job.
