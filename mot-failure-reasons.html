What Actually Fails an MOT

Just over a quarter of cars fail first time. The government publishes exactly what they fail on, and the answer is not what most people expect.

The short answer

  • 26.75% of cars fail first time, with 2.39 defects each.
  • Lamps cause the most failures — usually a bulb.

Overall initial failure rate: 26.75%. The average failed test carries 2.39 defects, so cars that fail rarely fail on one thing.

The number that matters more

Of that 26.75%, a dangerous defect was recorded on 7.25% of tests and a major one on 19.50%. A dangerous defect means the car should not be driven away.

And dangerous defects are concentrated almost entirely in two places:

CategoryShare of all dangerous defects
Tyres62.69%

Tyres account for nearly two-thirds of every dangerous defect recorded in the country. Lamps cause the most failures; tyres cause the most danger. Those are different problems and they get treated as the same one.

Lamps are the top failure and the cheapest fix

A tenth of all tests fail on lamps, reflectors and electrical equipment, and almost none of it is dangerous — 1.24% of dangerous defects against 29.11% of major ones.

It is also the category you can check yourself in five minutes on a dark evening: dipped and main beam, both sides; sidelights; brake lights, including the high-level one; indicators front, rear and side repeaters; reversing light; number plate lights; fog lights front and rear.

A blown bulb is the single most common reason a car fails its MOT in Great Britain, and it is usually a few pounds and ten minutes. Check them the week before.

Why local cars fail on different things

The national figures say what fails. What decides which of them bites a particular car is how that car is used, and around here that is short journeys.

Hackbridge, Carshalton, Wallington and Mitcham are commuter suburbs with stations on them. A great many local cars cover a mile to a station car park and back, twice a day, and never reach proper running temperature. That pattern pushes certain categories up:

  • Brakes. Discs surface-rust between uses and get scored by it, and rear calipers and handbrake mechanisms seize on cars that barely move. Brakes are 27% of dangerous defects.
  • Tyres. Low-mileage cars age their tyres out rather than wearing them out. Plenty of tread, perished sidewalls — and tyres are 63% of dangerous defects.
  • Suspension. Kerbing in streets parked solid on both sides does more to bushes and track rods than motorway miles ever will. Second biggest category nationally.
  • Noise, emissions and leaks. Exhausts corrode from the inside when condensation never dries out of them.

So the advice that suits a high-mileage car — watch the tread, watch the pads — is close to the wrong advice here. On a low-mileage local car the things that fail are the things that rot and seize, and those are found by looking rather than by watching a number.

What to check before you book

  1. Every lamp, in the dark. Top failure category nationally, and the cheapest thing on this list to put right.
  2. Tyres, including the sidewalls. Tread depth and cracking. Nearly two-thirds of dangerous defects.
  3. Wipers and washers. Visibility is 4.4% of tests, and torn blades or an empty washer bottle are a free fail.
  4. Number plate and VIN legible. Small category, entirely avoidable.
  5. Listen for a knock over bumps. Suspension is the second biggest category and the commonest local symptom.

Your questions answered

What percentage of cars fail the MOT?

26.75% of class 3 and 4 vehicles fail the initial test, on DVSA’s published figures for 2026–27 Q1. The average failure carries 2.39 defects.

What is the most common MOT failure?

Lamps, reflectors and electrical equipment — 9.98% of all tests, a quarter of all defects recorded. Usually a bulb.

What is the difference between major and dangerous?

Both fail. Dangerous means the car should not be driven away at all. A dangerous defect was recorded on 7.25% of tests, and tyres account for 62.69% of them.

My car does hardly any miles. Is it more likely to pass?

Not necessarily, and that surprises people. Low-mileage cars fail on corroded discs, seized rear calipers and perished tyres — things that happen with time and standing rather than with distance.

Can you fix a failure the same day?

Often, if it is a bulb, a wiper or a tyre. Bigger items depend on parts. We will tell you what it needs and what it costs before doing anything.
