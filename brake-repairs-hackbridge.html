Brake Repairs for Hackbridge drivers

The roads immediately around us

Across the borough, DfT collision data published through crashview.co.uk records 11,569 collisions in Sutton, 14,236 casualties and 1,588 serious ones, with the A232 at 1,438 and the A217 at 1,025 the two that carry the most. Hackbridge sits off that main network rather than on it, and the local driving is the slow sort: London Road, the parade, the level crossing, and residential streets parked solid on both sides.

Slow driving is not kind to suspension and steering. It is kerbs that do the damage — pulling into a gap barely longer than the car, squeezing past something double-parked, mounting a kerb to let a delivery lorry through. A hard kerb strike at five miles an hour puts a shock into a wishbone, a track rod and a wheel bearing that thirty miles of dual carriageway never will.

Add the building traffic from the Felnex and New Mill sites and the surface of the local roads has taken a beating with it. Potholes, patched trenches and sunken ironwork all land through the same components.

So the symptom we hear most around here is not a vague motorway wobble. It is a knock over the bumps on a residential street, or a car that has pulled to one side ever since a kerb.

What a kerb actually does

  • Bends a track rod or moves the tracking. The car pulls, and the new tyre wears across its width.
  • Shocks a wheel bearing. It may not hum for months, and then it does.
  • Splits a suspension bush. The knock over bumps that everybody describes as coming from the road rather than the car.
  • Buckles the rim slightly. A vibration that people blame on tyre balance and chase for a year.
  • Marks the sidewall. If the cords show, the tyre is finished regardless of tread depth.

So on anything that has been kerbed properly we would rather look at the whole corner than reset the tracking and hand it back.

Mechanical

Brake Repairs: what’s involved

When you press the pedal, the master cylinder pushes brake fluid down the pipes and flexible hoses to the callipers, which clamp the pads against the discs. The friction turns your speed into heat. Pads and discs wear with every stop, so they are the parts most often replaced, but callipers seize, slide pins corrode, hoses perish and brake fluid absorbs moisture over time.

Most brake problems give plenty of warning: a squeal from the pad wear indicator, a vibration through the pedal, a pull to one side or a pedal that feels soft. Dealing with them early usually keeps the job to pads and discs. Left alone, a worn pad can score the disc down to the metal backing plate and a sticking calliper can overheat the whole corner, which costs more and makes the car less safe to stop.

Signs your brakes need attention

Squealing when braking

Many pads have a small metal tab that squeals against the disc once the friction material is nearly worn. Some cars use an electronic wear sensor that lights a warning on the dash instead.

Grinding or scraping

A harsh grinding usually means the pad material has gone and the steel backing plate is contacting the disc. Stop driving as soon as it is safe; the disc will almost certainly need replacing.

Vibration through the pedal or steering

A pulsing pedal or shaking wheel under braking is usually uneven disc thickness, often described as warped discs. It tends to be worst when braking from higher speeds.

Car pulls to one side

A pull under braking points to a seized calliper piston, sticking slide pins or a collapsed flexible hose on one side. It can also be contaminated pads on one wheel.

Soft, spongy or long pedal

Air in the system, old fluid that has absorbed moisture, a leak or a failing master cylinder can all make the pedal travel further than normal. This needs checking straight away.

Hot wheel or burning smell

If one wheel is much hotter than the others after a drive, or you smell hot brakes, a calliper or handbrake mechanism may be dragging.

Brake warning light

The red brake light can mean low fluid level, which usually follows pad wear or a leak. An amber light may be a pad wear sensor, ABS or electronic parking brake fault.

Handbrake not holding

Excessive lever travel or a car that rolls on a slope suggests stretched cables, seized rear callipers or worn rear shoes or pads.

When should brakes be replaced?

There is no fixed mileage for pads and discs because wear depends on how and where you drive. Stop-start town traffic wears brakes far faster than motorway miles, and heavier cars and SUVs get through them quicker. We replace pads when they reach the manufacturer's minimum thickness and discs when they are below the minimum thickness stamped on the disc, or are badly scored, lipped or corroded. Brake fluid is different: it is replaced on a time basis because it absorbs moisture even if the car is rarely used.

  • Front pads: commonly 20,000–40,000 miles, less in heavy town use
  • Discs: often every second set of pads, or sooner if scored or below minimum thickness
  • Brake fluid: most manufacturers specify every 2 years, regardless of mileage
  • Rear brakes usually last longer than the fronts, but rear discs on low-mileage cars often need replacing because of corrosion
  • Any pull, vibration, noise or change in pedal feel should be checked promptly

How we repair your brakes

  1. Check-in and road testWe talk through what you have noticed and, where it is safe, drive the car to feel the pedal, listen for noise and check for pulling or vibration.
  2. Wheels off inspectionWe measure pad thickness and disc thickness, check for scoring and lipping, and inspect callipers, slide pins, hoses, pipes and the handbrake.
  3. Fluid testWe test the brake fluid's moisture content and check the level and condition in the reservoir.
  4. Written quoteYou get a written quote showing exactly what needs replacing and what can wait. Nothing is done without your approval.
  5. Replace and serviceWe fit the new discs and pads, clean the hub faces so the discs sit true, and clean and lubricate slide pins with the correct grease. Cars with an electronic parking brake are put into service mode with diagnostic equipment so the rear callipers can be wound back safely.
  6. Bleed and fluid changeWhere a fluid change or hydraulic work is needed, we bleed the system with fresh fluid of the correct specification and check for leaks.
  7. Torque and checksCalliper bolts and wheel nuts are tightened to the manufacturer's torque settings and the pedal is checked before the car moves.
  8. Road test and handoverWe road test to confirm the car stops straight and smoothly, then explain what we did and how to bed the new pads in over the first few miles.

Brake parts we fit

Brakes are not the place to save a few pounds on unknown parts. We fit OE or OE-equivalent pads, discs, callipers and hoses from established brake manufacturers such as Brembo, TRW, ATE, Textar and Pagid, matched to your car's exact specification, including disc size and wear sensor type. Brake fluid is the grade your manufacturer specifies, usually DOT 4. Discs and pads are always replaced in axle pairs so the car brakes evenly.

  • OE or OE-equivalent brake pads with new fitting hardware where supplied
  • Discs matched to your car's diameter and thickness specification
  • Pad wear sensors where the car uses them
  • New or reconditioned callipers and flexible hoses when required
  • Brake fluid to manufacturer specification, usually DOT 4

If it’s left

Worn pads that reach the backing plate will damage the discs, turning a pads-only job into pads and discs. A sticking calliper can overheat the brake, boil the fluid and wear the pads unevenly. Old, moisture-laden fluid lowers the boiling point and can corrode internal parts. Most importantly, worn or unbalanced brakes increase your stopping distance.
