When the Replacement Does Not Match

One new alloy among three old ones. A replacement trim that is a different shade. There is an established way this gets settled, and almost nobody is told about it.

The rule of thumb nobody mentions

Where a part cannot be matched to the rest of a set, the Financial Ombudsman Service describes an approach of paying for the damaged part plus half the cost of replacing the rest of the set.

The logic is that replacing the whole set leaves you better off than before the accident, and replacing only one leaves you worse off. Splitting the difference on the remainder is the compromise.

SituationTypical outcome
A matching part is availableIt is fitted. No issue.
A reasonably close part is availableThat is normally considered enough
Nothing matches — one of a setThe damaged part, plus around half the cost of the rest
Paint cannot be matchedA respray, or the cash equivalent

Where this comes up

  • Alloy wheels. The classic case. One kerbed wheel on a set of four, a discontinued design, and no match available.
  • Trim and mouldings. Faded by years of sun, so a new one is visibly darker even in the correct colour.
  • Headlights. One new clear unit beside one yellowed with age.
  • Paint. Where the original has faded beyond what blending can bridge.

What to do about it

  1. Raise it before the work is signed off. Much easier than afterwards.
  2. Ask what was searched. Whether a matching part was looked for new, used and from the manufacturer, and what came back.
  3. Photograph the mismatch in daylight. Alongside the unmatched items, so the difference is visible in one frame.
  4. Ask for the set to be addressed. Naming the half-cost approach. It is their own trade body’s territory, not something you have invented.
  5. Complain and escalate if refused. This is well-trodden ground at the Ombudsman.

The honest caveat

A reasonably close match is normally considered sufficient, and "reasonably close" does a lot of work in that sentence. If a replacement is marginally different in a way you notice and most people would not, you are unlikely to get far.

The argument is strongest where the difference is obvious to anyone looking — one bright wheel among three dull ones, a headlight that is visibly a different colour from its partner.

What we do about it

On alloys we refurbish rather than replace wherever the wheel allows it, which sides the problem entirely: a refurbished wheel can be finished to match its neighbours, and a new one cannot be made older.

Where a part genuinely cannot be matched we say so at the estimate rather than at handover, so the conversation with your insurer happens before the car is in pieces.

Your questions answered

Can I insist on all four wheels being done?

You can ask. The usual landing point is the damaged one plus a contribution toward the rest, rather than the full set, because a full set would leave you better off than before the accident.

They fitted a used part. Is that allowed?

Generally second-hand parts are not considered fair unless the car is very old, a classic, or a limited edition where new parts are not available. See the separate page on that.

The new bumper is a slightly different white.

That is a paint matching question rather than a parts one, and it usually has a better answer: blending into the neighbouring panels. If it was not blended, that is the complaint to make.

Does this apply if I paid privately?

The Ombudsman approach is about insurers. Paying privately, it is a contract matter between you and the bodyshop — which is why we tell you before the work if a part cannot be matched.
