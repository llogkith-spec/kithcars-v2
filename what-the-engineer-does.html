What the Insurance Engineer Actually Does

They work for the insurer, not for you, and that is not a criticism — it is the single most useful thing to understand about the stage of the claim that decides what gets paid for.

The short answer

  • They are instructed by the insurer, not by you.
  • They check method, labour times, parts grade and whether it is economical to repair.

Who they are and who pays them

A motor engineer is a qualified assessor instructed by the insurer to look at the damage and the estimate and say whether the two match. Some are employed by the insurer, many work for assessment firms contracted to several.

They are not an independent arbiter between you and the insurer, and they are not inspecting the car on your behalf. Their job is to make sure the insurer pays for the damage from this accident, repaired by a reasonable method, in a reasonable time.

What they are actually checking

  • Does the damage match the account? A rear-end shunt that has damaged a front wing raises a question.
  • Is any of it pre-existing? Old damage on the same panel is excluded, and this is a common source of argument.
  • Is the method right? Repair against replace, and whether a panel that could be straightened is being replaced instead.
  • Are the labour times reasonable? Measured against published times for that operation on that vehicle.
  • Is the parts grade appropriate? Genuine, matching-quality or recycled, and what the policy entitles you to.
  • Is it economical to repair at all? This is where a write-off decision comes from.

Photo assessment versus in person

Most straightforward claims are now assessed from photographs, which is faster and works perfectly well for visible damage. It has one weakness, and it is the reason supplementaries exist: a photograph shows the face of a panel and nothing behind it.

An in-person inspection is more likely on larger claims, on suspected structural damage, and where the estimate and the photographs seem to disagree. If you think yours needs seeing rather than photographing, you can ask — particularly where there is a reason to think the damage goes deeper than it looks.

When they cut the estimate

It happens, and it is not automatically wrong. Sometimes there is a cheaper method that is genuinely correct, and a good bodyshop will accept that rather than argue for the sake of it.

What should not happen is the difference being absorbed by doing less work — skipping the blend into the next panel, filling over something that needed replacing, or not recalibrating a sensor that came out. That is where a pre-agreed network rate and a squeezed labour time quietly turn into a visibly worse repair.

  1. Ask for the reduction in writing. With the reason and the times they have applied.
  2. We respond with evidence. Photographs from strip-down, manufacturer method, published times.
  3. If it is still wrong, you complain. It is your claim and your policy. A complaint from the policyholder carries weight a bodyshop’s does not.

Your questions answered

Can I get my own independent engineer?

Yes. You pay for it, and on a disputed total loss or a significant structural disagreement it can be money well spent. On a bumper it is not.

The engineer says some damage is pre-existing.

Ask which, and ask what they based it on. Photographs from before the accident, service records or an MOT advisory history can settle it either way.

How long does the engineer stage take?

Anything from a day to a couple of weeks. Backlogs are the usual reason, and chasing as the policyholder genuinely helps.

Do you deal with the engineer or do I?

We do, from the estimate onward. You are better placed to chase authorisation when it stalls, because you are the customer and we are not.
