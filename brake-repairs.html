In the workshop

How we handle brake repair at Hackbridge

Every brake job here starts with numbers. Disc thickness is measured with a micrometer at several points around the face, taken from inside the wear lip, and compared against the minimum figure cast or stamped into the disc itself. Pad material is measured rather than eyeballed. Where the complaint is a judder, a dial gauge goes on the hub and runout is checked before anything is condemned, because rust or dirt behind the disc produces exactly the same symptom as a worn one. Fluid is tested for moisture content and the reading is written on your sheet alongside the level at which it should be renewed.

Fitting is where brakes are made or ruined. Hub faces are cleaned back to bare metal so the new disc sits flat, because a few hundredths of a millimetre of rust behind a disc is enough to bring the judder back within a few thousand miles. Slide pins are cleaned and go back with a rubber-compatible brake grease rather than copper grease, which attacks the pin seals and boots. Anti-rattle hardware is renewed where the kit supplies it. Carrier bolts and wheel fixings are tightened with a torque wrench rather than a rattle gun, and cars with electrically operated rear brakes are put into service mode and recalibrated afterwards.

We do not machine discs that are already close to their minimum thickness, and we do not fit new pads onto a disc that has fallen below it, because a pad cannot do its job against a surface that can no longer carry the heat. We do not top brake fluid up to disguise a leak. We do not sell four corners when two need doing. The quote follows the inspection and is priced on the discs, pads, sensors and hardware your car actually takes, and if a calliper is seized once the wheel is off, you are called before another penny is spent.

The cars we see

Town-driven hatchbacks and estates are the bulk of it, wearing their fronts quickly while the rear discs corrode from lack of work. Vans and pickups push weight through the front axle and get through pads far faster than their mileage suggests. Hybrids and electric cars bring the opposite problem, because regenerative braking means the friction brakes are barely used, so discs corrode and callipers seize. Larger SUVs run bigger and dearer discs, and most modern cars add an electric parking brake or a wear sensor to the job.

Brake Repairs: what’s involved

When you press the pedal, the master cylinder pushes brake fluid down the pipes and flexible hoses to the callipers, which clamp the pads against the discs. The friction turns your speed into heat. Pads and discs wear with every stop, so they are the parts most often replaced, but callipers seize, slide pins corrode, hoses perish and brake fluid absorbs moisture over time.

Most brake problems give plenty of warning: a squeal from the pad wear indicator, a vibration through the pedal, a pull to one side or a pedal that feels soft. Dealing with them early usually keeps the job to pads and discs. Left alone, a worn pad can score the disc down to the metal backing plate and a sticking calliper can overheat the whole corner, which costs more and makes the car less safe to stop.

Signs your brakes need attention

Squealing when braking

Many pads have a small metal tab that squeals against the disc once the friction material is nearly worn. Some cars use an electronic wear sensor that lights a warning on the dash instead.

Grinding or scraping

A harsh grinding usually means the pad material has gone and the steel backing plate is contacting the disc. Stop driving as soon as it is safe; the disc will almost certainly need replacing.

Vibration through the pedal or steering

A pulsing pedal or shaking wheel under braking is usually uneven disc thickness, often described as warped discs. It tends to be worst when braking from higher speeds.

Car pulls to one side

A pull under braking points to a seized calliper piston, sticking slide pins or a collapsed flexible hose on one side. It can also be contaminated pads on one wheel.

Soft, spongy or long pedal

Air in the system, old fluid that has absorbed moisture, a leak or a failing master cylinder can all make the pedal travel further than normal. This needs checking straight away.

Hot wheel or burning smell

If one wheel is much hotter than the others after a drive, or you smell hot brakes, a calliper or handbrake mechanism may be dragging.

Brake warning light

The red brake light can mean low fluid level, which usually follows pad wear or a leak. An amber light may be a pad wear sensor, ABS or electronic parking brake fault.

Handbrake not holding

Excessive lever travel or a car that rolls on a slope suggests stretched cables, seized rear callipers or worn rear shoes or pads.

When should brakes be replaced?

There is no fixed mileage for pads and discs because wear depends on how and where you drive. Stop-start town traffic wears brakes far faster than motorway miles, and heavier cars and SUVs get through them quicker. We replace pads when they reach the manufacturer's minimum thickness and discs when they are below the minimum thickness stamped on the disc, or are badly scored, lipped or corroded. Brake fluid is different: it is replaced on a time basis because it absorbs moisture even if the car is rarely used.

  • Front pads: commonly 20,000–40,000 miles, less in heavy town use
  • Discs: often every second set of pads, or sooner if scored or below minimum thickness
  • Brake fluid: most manufacturers specify every 2 years, regardless of mileage
  • Rear brakes usually last longer than the fronts, but rear discs on low-mileage cars often need replacing because of corrosion
  • Any pull, vibration, noise or change in pedal feel should be checked promptly

If it’s left

Worn pads that reach the backing plate will damage the discs, turning a pads-only job into pads and discs. A sticking calliper can overheat the brake, boil the fluid and wear the pads unevenly. Old, moisture-laden fluid lowers the boiling point and can corrode internal parts. Most importantly, worn or unbalanced brakes increase your stopping distance.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about brake repair

Warped discs cause the judder

Discs very rarely warp. The shake almost always comes from uneven thickness or uneven pad material deposited across the disc face, which is why hub cleanliness and proper bedding in matter more than buying a dearer disc.

A squeal means worn out

It can be the wear indicator, but it is just as often dry slide pins, missing shims or glazed friction material. Replacing healthy pads because of a noise fixes nothing when the cause is a pin that has stopped sliding.

Cheap pads are a saving

Unbranded friction material is often acceptable cold and poor when hot, which is precisely the wrong way round. What is saved on a set of pads is small against what it costs in stopping distance.

Topping the fluid up is maintenance

A dropping reservoir level is information. Usually it means the pads have worn and the pistons have moved outwards, occasionally it means a leak, and topping it up removes the warning either way.

Questions

Brake Repairs: your questions answered

Can I supply my own brake parts?

We would rather you did not, on brakes in particular. We cannot stand behind a part we have not bought, we have no way of confirming where it came from, and braking components are a well-known target for counterfeiting. If cost is the concern, tell us your budget and we will price the job with parts we are prepared to guarantee.

What happens if you find something extra once the wheels are off?

We stop, call you and tell you what it is and what it adds to the figure. A seized calliper or a corroded pipe often cannot be seen until the wheel comes off, so it is a normal part of brake work rather than a nasty surprise. Nothing additional is fitted without your agreement.

Will the brakes pass an MOT afterwards?

Brake efficiency and imbalance are measured on a roller brake tester during the MOT, and correctly fitted pads and discs on a system with no leaks or seized parts pass comfortably. Freshly fitted pads need a few miles to bed in before they read their best, which is one reason the car is road tested before you get it back.

Is brake work guaranteed?

Yes. Twelve months on our labour, and behind that the discs, pads and callipers come with whatever cover their maker offers. If something does not feel right, bring the car back and we will look at it. A noise in the first week or two is worth a phone call rather than a worry, and we would sooner check it than leave you wondering.

Do I always need new discs when I replace the pads?

No. If the discs are above minimum thickness, not badly scored or lipped and run true, new pads alone are fine. We measure the discs and tell you honestly. Many cars need discs every second pad change.

Why do my new brakes feel different?

New pads need to bed in against the discs. For the first 100–200 miles, brake progressively and avoid heavy stops where you can. Braking will improve as the surfaces match up.

How often should brake fluid be changed?

Most manufacturers specify every two years. Brake fluid absorbs moisture from the air, which lowers its boiling point and can cause a soft pedal under heavy use. We can test the moisture content to show you where yours is.

My rear discs are rusty but the car has low mileage. Why?

Rear brakes do less work, so they do not get hot enough or used hard enough to keep the disc surface clean. Cars that sit a lot or do short trips often develop corrosion on the rear discs that affects braking and can fail an MOT.

Can you work on cars with an electronic handbrake?

Yes. Electronic parking brakes need to be put into service mode with diagnostic equipment before rear pads are changed, then recalibrated afterwards. We do this as part of the job.
