In the workshop

How we handle van servicing at Hackbridge

The first question at the desk is what the van actually does. A long-wheelbase van running motorway deliveries and a small one doing twelve drops a day in Croydon wear out in completely different places, and the second is the reason we ask whether the schedule the dash is counting down to really suits it. We also ask for the van empty if you can manage it, or at least to know what is in the back, because a loaded van cannot be raised safely and weight on the rear axle hides the play we are looking for in springs, bushes and bearings.

On the ramp the attention goes to the back end. Rear drums come off rather than being judged through the inspection hole, because shoes wear unevenly on a van that spends its life loaded, and a seized adjuster or a weeping wheel cylinder is both common and cheap to catch early. Tyres are checked against the plate for load rating as well as tread. On diesels we read soot loading and the history since the last completed regeneration along with the AdBlue system status, and we read the pattern rather than just the warning light. Sliding door rollers, hinges and locks are checked and lubricated while it is up.

What comes back to you is a list in three parts: done, needed before the van goes out again, and due by the next service. The third group is the useful one, because it lets you book the downtime instead of having it chosen for you. We will not force a regeneration purely to put a light out without establishing why the filter filled, and we will not sign off a service using an oil that does not carry the maker's approval, whatever happens to be on the shelf. Anything beyond the quoted service is priced and agreed on the phone before it happens.

The cars we see

Most of the work is mid-size panel vans: Transit Custom, Vivaro and its Peugeot and Citroën relatives, Trafic, Transporter and Vito, with smaller Caddys, Berlingos and Combos alongside them. Larger Sprinters and full-size Transits put more weight over the rear axle and carry heavier brakes to suit, and rear-wheel-drive versions behave differently again when empty. Crew vans, tippers and anything with a rack on the roof spend their lives near the plated weight. What a van is rated to carry, rather than what it looks like, decides the parts we fit to it.

Van Servicing: what’s involved

Vans work harder than most cars. They carry heavy loads, stop and start all day and often cover high mileage, which puts extra strain on brakes, suspension, tyres, the clutch and the engine oil. Most modern diesel vans also have a diesel particulate filter, and many use AdBlue, both of which suffer when a van spends its day on short urban runs.

A van service follows the manufacturer's schedule with the correct approved oil, but we also pay attention to load-related wear: rear brakes, springs and shock absorbers, tyre condition and load rating, and the sliding door, rear doors and their hinges. For self-employed tradespeople and small businesses, catching a worn part at a planned service is far cheaper than losing a working day to a breakdown.

Signs your van needs attention

Service indicator or overdue service

Vans on long-life schedules can still need oil sooner if they do lots of short trips or idling, so don't stretch past the maximum interval.

Brakes feel weaker when loaded

Longer stopping distances with a load, a spongy pedal or grinding noises point to worn pads, discs or rear drums and shoes.

Sagging or bouncing rear

A van that sits low at the back, bounces over bumps or bottoms out can have broken springs or worn shock absorbers.

DPF or AdBlue warnings

Stop-start work makes vans prone to blocked particulate filters, and AdBlue system faults can lead to a restricted-start countdown on the dash.

Clutch slipping or heavy

Revs rising without the van speeding up, especially uphill or loaded, is a sign of a worn clutch or dual mass flywheel.

Uneven tyre wear

Heavy loads and kerbing wear tyres quickly. Van tyres need the right load index, usually marked with a C for commercial.

Doors sticking or not latching

Sliding door rollers, hinges and locks wear with constant use. A door that won't latch is a security and safety problem.

When should a van be serviced?

Follow the manufacturer's service schedule, which for most light vans is based on time and mileage, whichever comes first, and use the service indicator as a guide. Vans that do mainly short urban trips, carry heavy loads or tow may benefit from an interim oil and filter change between full services. The MOT is due at three years old, then annually. We can book services and MOTs together to minimise time off the road.

  • Full service to the manufacturer's time or mileage schedule
  • Interim oil and filter change for high-mileage or heavy-use vans
  • First MOT at three years, then every 12 months
  • Brake and suspension checks at every service, especially if regularly loaded
  • Cambelt or timing chain at the manufacturer's interval, which varies by engine

How we service your van

  1. Check-inWe ask how the van is used, typical loads and any faults, and check the manufacturer's schedule and history.
  2. Oil and filtersWe change the engine oil to the manufacturer's approval and replace the oil filter, plus air, cabin and fuel filters when due.
  3. Brakes and suspensionWith the van on the lift we check pads, discs, drums and shoes, brake pipes and hoses, springs, shock absorbers and suspension bushes.
  4. Tyres, steering and underbodyWe check tyre tread, condition and load rating, steering joints, driveshafts, the exhaust and the underbody for corrosion and leaks.
  5. Diesel system checksWe scan for fault codes and check DPF soot load and AdBlue system status on vans that have them.
  6. Doors, lights and load areaWe check lights, wipers, mirrors, the horn, and the operation of the sliding and rear doors, locks and hinges.
  7. Record and reportWe reset the service indicator, update the service record and give you a written report with any advisories and quotes.
  8. Road test and handoverWe road test the van and hand it back with a clear explanation of anything that needs attention soon.

Replacement tyres are matched to the van's required load index.

If it’s left

A van that misses services tends to fail at the worst time, often loaded and on the way to a job. Worn brakes and weak suspension are more dangerous when carrying weight, and overloaded or worn tyres can fail. Neglected oil changes shorten the life of turbos, timing chains and particulate filters, which are expensive to replace on modern diesel vans.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about van servicing

Waiting for the service light

Variable schedules are calculated for steady running. A van doing short urban drops, with the engine idling between them, is contaminating its oil far faster than the counter on the dash assumes.

Any 5W-30 will do

A diesel with a particulate filter needs a low-ash oil carrying the maker's own approval, not just the right viscosity. The wrong oil ashes the filter up early and the bill arrives years later.

An MOT counts as a service

The test is a safety check on the day. Nothing is adjusted, no oil is changed and a van can pass it with filters that have not been touched in three years.

Car brakes and tyres fit fine

They will physically fit. They are not rated for the weight, and on a van that is regularly loaded the difference turns up in stopping distance and in how quickly they are worn out.

Van Servicing: your questions answered

Can you keep me working while the van is in?

Ask at the point of booking. A courtesy car is subject to availability and is a car rather than a van, so if you need load space, say so early and we will tell you honestly what can be arranged for your dates. We collect and deliver locally as well.

Will you stamp the service book?

Yes, and you also get an itemised invoice listing the oil specification, the parts fitted and everything checked, which is what a buyer or a finance company actually wants to see. Where a manufacturer keeps a digital service record, we will tell you at booking what can and cannot be updated on it.

My van is on lease or contract hire. Can you still service it?

Usually, but read the agreement first. Some contracts include maintenance through a named network, while others simply require servicing to schedule with matching-quality parts. Bring the paperwork, and we will work to whatever it asks for and give you the records to prove it was done.

What if you find something and I cannot do it all at once?

The quote is split so you can see what genuinely cannot wait, normally brakes, tyres and anything that would fail an MOT, and what can safely go to the next visit. Nothing extra is carried out until you have said yes to the price.

Can you MOT my van?

Class 4 covers vans and other goods vehicles up to 3,000kg design gross weight; heavier vans up to 3,500kg fall into Class 7. Your van's design gross weight is printed on the VIN plate. Quote us that number when you ring and we will tell you before you book which test applies and whether we can carry it out.

Does my van need an operator's licence?

For most businesses using vans at or under 3,500kg gross weight in Great Britain, no. An operator's licence is generally needed for goods vehicles over 3,500kg, and since 2022 also for vans over 2,500kg used for hire or reward internationally. Check the gov.uk guidance for your situation.

Will servicing at an independent garage affect my van's warranty?

No. Warranties can't be made conditional on dealer servicing. As long as the van is serviced to the manufacturer's schedule with matching-quality parts and the correct oil, the warranty remains valid.

Why does my diesel van keep getting DPF warnings?

Short trips and lots of idling stop the filter regenerating properly. Faulty sensors, EGR valves or injectors can also cause it. We check the cause at the service, and a regular run at steady speed helps.

Do you look after several vans for one business?

Yes. Businesses with more than one vehicle can use our fleet service, with priority booking, consolidated invoicing and records for each vehicle.
