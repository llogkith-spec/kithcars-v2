Headlight Restoration: what’s involved

Headlamp lenses stopped being glass decades ago. Modern units use moulded polycarbonate, which is light and tough enough to survive a stone strike, but on its own it degrades badly in sunlight. The factory answer is a thin hard coat applied to the outer face, usually a siloxane or acrylic layer carrying ultraviolet absorbers. That coating, not the plastic, is what keeps a new lamp clear.

The coating has a finite life. Ultraviolet light, heat from the bulb behind it, motorway grit, harsh traffic film removers and the brushes in a rolling car wash all wear it thin, and once it breaks down the polycarbonate underneath begins to oxidise. That is what produces the familiar yellow-brown tint, the milky cloudiness and the fine network of surface cracks known as crazing. Light output drops as the surface scatters the beam, and the pattern thrown onto the road loses its sharp cut-off long before the driver notices how dim things have become.

Signs your headlamps need restoring

Yellow or amber tint

The lens looks brown in daylight even after washing. This is oxidised polycarbonate, and no amount of cleaning will shift it.

Milky or foggy surface

A hazy film that feels slightly rough under a fingertip, worst on the upper face where the sun hits hardest.

Crazing

Very fine cracks across the outer surface, often visible only at an angle. They mean the hard coat has failed rather than simply worn.

Weak, scattered beam at night

The road ahead looks flat and the beam has no clean edge. A deteriorated lens can cut usable light output substantially.

Peeling coating

Patches where the original coating has lifted, leaving clear areas next to cloudy ones and a blotchy appearance.

An MOT comment about the lamp

Headlamps that are deteriorated badly enough to reduce light output are a defect, and a poor beam pattern will be noted at test.

Sandblasted lower edge

Cars that cover motorway miles pick up thousands of small stone strikes low on the lens, which pit the surface and dull it.

One lamp worse than the other

Common where a car is parked with one side facing the sun, or where one unit has been replaced at some point in its life.

When is restoration the right answer?

Restoration works when the damage is on the outer surface of an otherwise sound unit: yellowing, haze, light crazing, stone pitting. Address it as soon as the beam starts looking soft at night, because oxidation runs deeper the longer it is left and eventually cannot be sanded out without taking away too much material. It is also a sensible job before an MOT or before selling, since cloudy lamps make an otherwise tidy car look neglected from twenty feet away.

  • As soon as the lens looks yellow or hazy rather than clear
  • Before an MOT if light output or beam pattern looks poor
  • Typically from around five to eight years of age, sooner on a car parked outside
  • When the car is being prepared for sale
  • Not the right job at all if the unit is cracked, misted inside or has a failed reflector

How we restore a headlamp lens

  1. Assess the unitWe look at whether the fault is outside or inside. Condensation, a dull or peeling reflector, a damaged projector or a crack through the lens all rule restoration out, and we say so before taking any money.
  2. Clean and maskThe lens is washed and degreased, then the surrounding paint, bonnet edge, grille and trim are masked with tape so no abrasive touches bodywork.
  3. Cut back the failed coatingWet sanding starts with a coarser grade, commonly around 800, worked with plenty of water to remove the degraded coating and the oxidised layer beneath it.
  4. Refine through the gradesWe step through finer papers, typically 1200, 2000 and 3000, changing sanding direction at each stage so we can see when the previous grade's scratches have gone. Skipping a grade leaves marks that show through later.
  5. Machine polishA compound on a machine polisher brings the surface back to optical clarity and removes the last of the sanding haze.
  6. Prepare for coatingThe lens is cleaned with a suitable solvent wipe and dried thoroughly. Any residue left at this stage will show as a blemish under the clear coat.
  7. Apply UV-stable clear coatA two-pack clear or a dedicated UV hard coat is applied, which is the step that replaces the protection the sanding removed. Without it the bare polycarbonate will yellow again.
  8. Cure and inspectThe coating is left to cure properly before the car is handled, then the lens is checked in good light for runs, dust or missed areas.
  9. Beam check and handoverWe check the beam pattern and aim, unmask the car and explain how to look after the finish.

Materials and replacement units

The materials matter more than most people expect. We use graded wet-and-dry abrasives, a polishing compound suited to polycarbonate and a clear coat formulated with ultraviolet absorbers, because a general-purpose lacquer with no UV package will yellow along with the lens it is protecting. Where a unit is beyond saving, we source OE or OE-equivalent replacements carrying the correct E-mark for road use.

  • Wet-and-dry abrasive papers from around 800 through to 3000 grit
  • Polishing compound formulated for polycarbonate
  • Two-pack or dedicated UV-stable clear coat
  • Masking tape and film to protect paint and trim
  • OE or OE-equivalent replacement headlamp units where needed
  • Bulbs and lens seals where they are the underlying problem

If it’s left

A badly degraded lens is a safety matter before it is a cosmetic one, because scattered light reduces how far you can see on unlit roads and can dazzle oncoming drivers. Leaving it also narrows your options: once oxidation and crazing reach deep into the plastic, sanding far enough to clear them thins and distorts the lens, and the only honest answer becomes a new unit at several times the cost.

Headlight Restoration in Thornton Heath: your questions answered

What is crazing, and does it mean the lens is beyond saving?

Crazing is a network of very fine cracks across the outer surface, often only visible at an angle, and it means the factory hard coat has failed rather than simply worn thin. Light crazing sands out. Deep crazing does not, because clearing it would thin and distort the lens, and then a replacement unit is the honest answer.

The car is on the street all week. Will this last?

A UV-stable clear coat over a properly refined surface should give several years even on a street-parked car, though not as long as on one kept under cover, and it is not permanent. Keeping solvent cleaners and close-range pressure washing off the lenses makes a real difference to how long you get.

Can you quote before I bring the car over?

We can give you the starting price, but not a firm quote, because the only way to know whether the fault is on the surface or inside the unit is to look at it. That assessment is part of the job and we will tell you if restoration is not the right answer before taking any money.

Why does a restored headlight go yellow again?

Because sanding and polishing take away whatever remains of the factory hard coat, leaving bare polycarbonate exposed to sunlight. A lens finished at the polishing stage looks superb for a few months and then hazes faster than it did originally. The clear coat is not an upsell, it is the part of the job that makes the result last.

My lens is cracked. Can you still restore it?

No. A crack goes through the material and lets water in, and abrasives cannot close it. The same applies to a lamp with condensation inside, a reflector whose coating has burnt or peeled, or a broken mounting tab. Those units need replacing, and we would rather tell you that than take payment for a polish that solves nothing.

There is water inside the lamp. What causes that?

Usually a perished lens seal, a blocked or missing breather, or a crack in the housing behind the visible surface. Some units can be dried and resealed successfully, others cannot because the seam is bonded at manufacture. We will open up the diagnosis before committing you to anything.

How long does the finish last?

With a proper UV-stable clear coat applied over a clean, fully refined surface, expect several years of service, and longer on a car kept in a garage or under cover. Life is shortened by constant sun exposure, high-pressure washing right at the lens and strong solvent-based cleaners. Washing normally and avoiding traffic film remover on the lamps will get the best out of it.

Is it cheaper than fitting new headlamps?

Considerably, in most cases. A single genuine headlamp unit for a modern car with projector or LED lighting can run into several hundred pounds before fitting and, on many vehicles, coding. Restoration is worth doing first whenever the unit itself is sound, and nothing is lost by having us look at it and tell you which way to go.
