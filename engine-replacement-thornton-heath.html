Engine Replacement: what’s involved

Replacing an engine is a big decision, and it should only happen once the failure is confirmed. Some problems that sound terminal, such as a misfire, heavy smoke or a no-start, can be injectors, coils, a turbo or a head gasket. Genuine engine failure usually means internal mechanical damage: bent valves and damaged pistons after a timing failure, spun bearings from oil starvation, water ingestion that bends connecting rods, or overheating that cracks the head or block.

When the engine is beyond economical repair, a replacement engine is often cheaper than a full rebuild and can give a sound car many more years. The replacement must match your engine code so it fits the car's mountings, wiring and engine management. We transfer ancillary parts, renew the seals and wearing parts that are easy to reach with the engine out, and set everything up before the car goes back on the road.

When an engine may need replacing

Deep knocking from the bottom end

A heavy knock that rises with revs, often after low oil pressure, usually means worn or spun crankshaft bearings.

Timing belt or chain failure

If the timing has broken or jumped on an interference engine, several valves are usually bent and pistons can be damaged. We check the extent before advising.

Water ingestion

Driving through deep water can draw water into the cylinders. The engine stops abruptly, and connecting rods can bend.

Severe overheating

An engine driven while overheating can warp or crack the cylinder head or block, often with coolant mixing into the oil.

Low compression across cylinders

Poor compression in several cylinders from worn rings or bores leads to smoke, oil consumption and lack of power.

Metal in the oil

Metallic particles in the oil or sump usually mean internal parts are breaking up.

Seized engine

An engine that will not turn at all on the starter or by hand has internal damage.

When is replacement the right choice?

Replacement makes sense when the engine has confirmed internal damage and the rest of the car is worth keeping. We compare three options honestly: repairing the existing engine, fitting a replacement, or not proceeding if the car's value does not justify the cost. The age and condition of the gearbox, body and suspension matter too. If a head rebuild or new timing kit will solve the problem, we will say so rather than recommend a whole engine.

  • Confirmed bottom-end damage such as spun bearings
  • Multiple bent valves with piston or head damage after a timing failure
  • Bent connecting rods after water ingestion
  • Cracked block or head after severe overheating
  • When the rest of the car justifies the investment

How we replace an engine

  1. Confirm the failureWe use compression and leak-down tests, fault codes, a borescope inspection of the cylinders and oil checks to confirm what has actually failed.
  2. Options and written quoteWe explain repair versus replacement, reconditioned versus used engines, and what warranty the engine supplier provides, then quote in writing.
  3. Source the right engineWe source an engine with the same engine code and check its mileage and condition. Used engines are checked for compression where the supplier allows.
  4. Remove the old engineFluids are drained and the engine removed, with or without the gearbox depending on the car.
  5. Prepare the replacementAncillaries are transferred, and we replace seals, gaskets and wearing parts that are easy to reach now. On used engines with unknown history we recommend a new cambelt kit.
  6. Fit and connectThe engine is fitted with new mounts where needed, connected and filled with oil, coolant and any fluids disturbed, to manufacturer specification.
  7. First start and checksWe prime the oil system before first start, check oil pressure, leaks and fault codes, and set up any adaptations. Diesel injector codes are programmed if injectors change.
  8. Road test and handoverWe road test, recheck levels and leaks once cool, and give you the new engine number and running-in advice.

Engine Replacement in Thornton Heath: your questions answered

My engine died in a Thornton Heath bus lane. Will I get a fine while waiting for recovery?

A genuine breakdown is usually treated differently from parking, but it is worth keeping evidence such as a photo and your recovery booking. Call us with the exact location and we will get the car moved as soon as we can.

Is a used engine safe to fit in an older car from West Thornton?

A good used engine from an established supplier, with known mileage and the correct engine code, is a sensible choice for many older cars. We recommend a new cambelt kit on used engines of unknown history, and explain the supplier's warranty before you commit.

How do you check whether my engine really needs replacing?

We use compression and leak-down tests, a borescope to inspect the cylinders, fault code readings and an oil check for metal or coolant. That tells us whether it is a repairable fault or genuine internal damage.

Reconditioned or used: which is better?

A reconditioned engine has been rebuilt with new wearing parts, so it costs more but starts fresh. A good used engine is cheaper and suits older or lower-value cars. We explain the options and the warranty each supplier offers so you can decide.

What warranty do I get?

Our labour carries our 12-month workmanship warranty. The engine itself is covered by the supplier's warranty, which varies between suppliers and between reconditioned and used units. We tell you exactly what applies before you commit.

Do I need to tell the DVLA?

Your V5C records the engine number, and an engine change is one of the changes you must tell DVLA about, so update it with the new engine number after the work. We give you the details you need.

Is it worth replacing the engine in an older car?

It depends on the car's condition and value, not just its age. A well-kept car with a sound body, gearbox and suspension can be worth it. If the numbers do not add up, we will tell you.

Will I need to run in the new engine?

For a reconditioned engine, yes: gentle driving and varied revs for the first few hundred miles, plus an early oil check. We give you specific advice for your engine at handover.
