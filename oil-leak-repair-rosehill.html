Why the wet patch is never where the leak starts

Rosehill driving includes a decent share of open road. The A217 along Bishopsford Road and the A297 up St Helier Avenue are dual carriageways where traffic moves, and that airflow under the car is what makes oil leaks so misleading. At thirty or forty miles an hour, oil escaping at the front of the engine is driven rearwards along the sump, over the bellhousing and onto the exhaust before it ever falls. Park by the hospital or the old Gaumont parade and the patch appears under the middle or back of the car.

This is where money gets wasted. Replace the part nearest the wet patch and there is a fair chance the leak is unchanged, because the oil was only passing through. On a higher-mileage car there may also be more than one source at once, so even a correct repair can leave something still dripping and the impression that nothing was achieved. Neither problem can be solved by looking harder at a dirty engine.

The only reliable method is to remove the history. We steam clean the engine and underside so no old oil remains anywhere, add a measured dose of ultraviolet dye to the sump, and run the car or return it to you for a few days of normal use. Under a UV lamp the dye glows at the precise point it escapes, and an inspection camera reaches up the bellhousing and behind the timing cover where nothing can be seen directly. Where there are two sources we tell you which matters now.

Nine minutes down Wrythe Lane and Hackbridge Road makes this easy to split across two short visits rather than one long one. If the level is low, say so when you call and we will talk about collection instead. After any repair the area is cleaned again, the car is brought up to temperature and road tested, and it goes back on the ramp so we can confirm it is dry before you collect it, with our 3-month workmanship warranty behind the work.

Oil Leak Repair: what’s involved

Engine oil is held in by three different kinds of seal: static gaskets between fixed parts such as the sump and cam cover, rotating shaft seals at the ends of the crankshaft and camshafts, and O-rings around filter housings, coolers and sensors. All of them harden with heat and age, and all of them work against whatever pressure exists inside the crankcase.

That pressure matters more than most drivers expect. The breather system is meant to draw blow-by gas back into the intake and keep the crankcase slightly below atmospheric pressure. Block the breather or jam its valve and pressure builds until it finds the weakest seal, which is why a freshly fitted gasket can be pushed out within weeks if only the gasket was addressed. Oil also travels: airflow under a moving car carries it rearwards, so the wet area is rarely where it started.

Signs you have an oil leak

Spots on the drive

Fresh engine oil is light amber and darkens with use, so a drip that starts pale and turns brown is usually engine oil. Where it lands says little on its own, since airflow moves it before it falls.

Burning smell after a drive

Oil falling onto a hot manifold or turbo burns off with a sharp smell, sometimes with smoke rising from under the bonnet while you sit at lights. On many cars that points at the cam cover.

Level dropping between services

Needing to add oil regularly with nothing obvious underneath can mean a leak burning off before it reaches the ground, or oil being consumed internally. Those two need separating before anything is replaced.

Gritty black film on the engine or subframe

Oil attracts road dust, so a leak of any age shows as a greasy coating rather than a clean wet patch. The older the leak, the wider the area it covers.

Oil warning light

A low level warning is a reason to stop and check the dipstick. A red oil pressure light is different and means stop immediately, because the engine is not being lubricated.

Smoke after the car has been standing

Oil that pooled on the undertray during a drive cooks off the next time the exhaust warms through, often a day later.

Oil near the timing cover

A leaking crankshaft or camshaft seal behind the cover soaks the cambelt, and rubber and oil do not mix. Anything wet in this area needs looking at promptly.

Clutch slipping on a high-mileage car

A failed rear crankshaft seal or gearbox input seal puts oil onto the friction plate, and the clutch starts to give way under load long before the friction material is worn out.

Seep or leak: when does it need fixing?

Not every damp gasket is a job. A seep leaves a surface oily but never forms a drop, and the dipstick stays where you left it. Plenty of engines past a hundred thousand miles live like that for years without trouble. It becomes a leak when oil drips, when you are topping up between services, or when it lands somewhere it must not: a cambelt, a clutch, an exhaust, an alternator or a rubber bush. Those cases need attention whatever the rate of loss looks like.

  • Check the oil level at least monthly and before any long journey
  • Any oil reaching a cambelt, clutch or exhaust needs fixing now, whatever the size
  • Topping up more than around half a litre between services is a leak, not consumption
  • Have a known seep re-inspected at every service to see whether it is getting worse
  • Renew crankshaft and camshaft seals while the timing belt is already off, not afterwards

How we find and fix an oil leak

  1. Go through the historyHow much oil you are adding, how often, what it smells like and when the spots appear all narrow the field before the car goes on the ramp.
  2. Check the oil itselfWe note the level, the grade in the engine and whether the oil has been diluted with fuel or contaminated with coolant, because that changes what we are dealing with.
  3. Degrease the areaThe engine and underside are steam cleaned so no old oil remains. Hunting a source through years of accumulated coating is guesswork.
  4. Dye and driveA measured dose of ultraviolet dye goes into the sump, then the car is run here or returned to you for a few days, so the leak shows itself under the conditions that normally cause it.
  5. Inspect under ultraviolet lightWith a UV lamp and filter glasses the dye glows at the exact point it escapes. An inspection camera reaches behind the timing cover and up the bellhousing where nothing else can see.
  6. Measure crankcase pressureWe test the breather and separator, because a blocked system pressurises the crankcase and forces oil past seals that are otherwise perfectly serviceable.
  7. Quote by sourceYou get a written price for the leak we have found and, where there is more than one, a straight opinion on which matters now and which can be left.
  8. Repair and resealMating faces are cleaned back to clean metal, new gaskets or seals fitted dry or with the specified sealant, and everything torqued to figure. Shaft seals are pressed in square with the correct drift rather than tapped home.
  9. Recheck after a runThe area is cleaned again, the car brought up to temperature and road tested, then put back on the ramp so we can confirm the repair is dry before handing it over.

Seals and gaskets we fit

Most leaks are cured with gaskets, seals and O-rings rather than large assemblies, and we use OE or OE-equivalent parts from suppliers such as Elring, Victor Reinz and Corteco. Crankshaft and camshaft seals are ordered against the manufacturer's part number, because lip design and spring tension vary between engines that otherwise look identical. Where a plastic filter housing or cam cover has warped or split, it is replaced rather than sealed. Oil is topped up with the correct Castrol grade for your engine.

If it’s left

The risk is rarely the oil on the tarmac, it is where the oil lands. A soaked cambelt can shed its teeth, a contaminated clutch gives way, oil on a hot exhaust is a fire risk, and oil on a suspension bush or driveshaft gaiter destroys it. A leak also drops the level between checks, and running low damages bearings and the turbo well before any warning light appears.

Oil Leak Repair in Rosehill: your questions answered

The oil is on the gearbox, so is that where the leak is?

Not necessarily, and on a car used on the A217 and A297 it often is not. Airflow carries oil rearwards, so a cam cover or front crankshaft seal leak can coat the bellhousing and look exactly like a gearbox fault. Cleaning everything and running the car again is what separates the two.

How can you see behind the timing cover without stripping the engine?

With an inspection camera and a UV lamp. The dye glows under ultraviolet light, and a camera on a flexible probe gets into the space behind the cover and up the bellhousing. It does not replace stripping where a repair is needed, but it means we only strip once we know what we are fixing.

I only have an hour free. Is that enough to make a start?

It is enough for the first half. The clean and the dye dose can be done while you wait, then you drive the car normally for a few days and bring it back for the inspection. Splitting it that way suits people working shifts nearby, and nothing is lost by the gap.

Will a stop-leak additive work?

They work by swelling rubber, which can slow a small seep for a while. They do nothing for a hardened gasket, a warped housing or a scored shaft, and the swelling acts on every seal in the engine rather than only the one leaking. Finding the source and repairing it is the better use of the money.

Why clean the engine before diagnosing?

Because oil runs. A cam cover weeping at the back can coat the gearbox and look exactly like a bellhousing leak. Cleaning everything first, then running the car, means the only fresh oil we find has to be coming from the real source.

How can I tell engine oil from other fluids?

Engine oil is amber to black and feels slippery between the fingers. Coolant is bright pink, blue or green and smells sweet. Gearbox and power steering fluid are usually red and much thinner, and brake fluid is nearly clear. If you are unsure, slide a sheet of cardboard under the car overnight and bring it in with you.

Can an oil leak fail the MOT?

A leak bad enough to form drops can be recorded as a major defect for posing an environmental hazard, which is a fail. Oil on brake or steering components fails outright. A light seep with no drips normally passes, though the tester may record it as an advisory.

Can a small leak just be kept an eye on?

Often that is the sensible choice, particularly when the repair involves removing a gearbox or a timing cover. We note the exact source, mark the level and look again at your next service. If nothing has moved, nothing needs spending.
