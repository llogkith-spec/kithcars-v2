Do I Have to Use My Insurer’s Approved Repairer?

No. The car is yours and you decide who repairs it. Insurers steer you towards their own network because it costs them less, and the script they use can sound like a rule. It is not one.

The short answer

  • No. You choose who repairs your car. Your insurer cannot overrule that.
  • The approved list is a discount arrangement, not a quality standard.
  • You may give up their repair guarantee, and sometimes a courtesy car.
  • The real catch: they authorise at their own labour rate and leave you the shortfall. Settle that before the car is booked in.

What the approved network actually is

An insurer’s “approved repairer” list is a commercial arrangement. Bodyshops on it have agreed discounted labour rates and, in most cases, to use the insurer’s preferred parts and estimating method in exchange for a steady flow of work. It is a supply contract. It is not a quality badge, and no regulator audits entry to it.

That does not make approved shops bad — plenty are excellent. It does mean the list was drawn up to serve the insurer’s costs, and the discount has to come from somewhere: usually the labour time allowed on the job, and sometimes the grade of parts specified.

What your insurer can and cannot do

Your insurer cannot force you to use their repairer. What they can do is write terms into the policy about what changes if you do not, and those terms are enforceable because you agreed to them when you bought the cover.

What they may doWhether it is usually enforceable
Recommend their own network stronglyYes — and they will
Refuse to let you use your own repairerNo
Apply a higher excess if you go elsewhereYes, if the policy says so
Decline to guarantee work they did not arrangeYes — we guarantee our own
Withhold a courtesy carYes, if the courtesy car is tied to their network
Pay only up to their own assessed costYes — this is the common one

That last row is the one that matters in practice. Rather than refusing outright, the insurer agrees the repair but authorises it at the rate they pay their own network. If your chosen bodyshop charges more, you can be asked to cover the gap. Check it before the car is booked in, not after.

What to say when they push

  1. Say it plainly. “I am using my own repairer. Please note that on the claim and send me the authorisation.” You do not need to justify the choice.
  2. Ask the cost question directly. “What labour rate are you authorising, and will there be any shortfall for me to pay?” Get the answer in writing or by email.
  3. Ask what you lose. Usually the courtesy car and their repair guarantee. Ask whether your excess changes.
  4. Give them our details. They will want the bodyshop name, address and contact. We deal with the engineer and the estimate from there.

If the handler insists it is not allowed, ask them to point you to the clause in your policy wording that says so. That usually ends the conversation.

Your questions answered

My insurer says the warranty on my car will be affected. Is that right?

That is a different thing and it is usually overstated. A manufacturer warranty covers defects in manufacture, not accident damage. Repairing crash damage at an independent bodyshop does not void it. What can matter is a separate anti-corrosion or paint warranty on a newer car, which may require the work to be done to a particular standard — worth checking if the car is under three years old.

Will it slow the claim down?

Usually not by much. The engineer still has to inspect and authorise either way. The one thing that does add time is the back and forth if the insurer authorises less than the job takes, which is why we settle the rate before starting.

Can I change to you after their garage has already started?

Yes, though it gets messier. The work already done has to be paid for and the car moved. If the repair has gone wrong, that is a complaint against the insurer and their repairer, and it is worth putting in writing before you move the car.

Do you handle the claim for me?

We deal with the estimate, the engineer and the supplementary. You still make the first call to report it, because the policy is yours. For non-fault claims we can take more of it off your hands.
