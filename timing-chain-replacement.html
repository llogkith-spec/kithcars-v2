How we handle timing chain replacement at Hackbridge

Chain work is booked in as a block rather than squeezed between other jobs, because the engine sits stripped and open. Before anything comes apart we listen from stone cold, in the first two or three seconds after the key turns, which is when a worn chain and a drained tensioner give themselves away. We scan for stored correlation and camshaft adjuster codes and read the live deviation figures where the engine reports them. If those numbers sit inside the manufacturer's tolerance and the noise is coming from somewhere else, we say so, and you have saved yourself a very large bill.

Once the job is on, cleanliness decides how long the repair lasts. The sump and the cambox are covered while the old chain comes out, and anything that has shed material gets followed up: on engines where broken guide plastic is known to travel, we look at the pick-up and strainer rather than hoping. Chain, guides and tensioner go in as a set, with sprockets and adjusters where they are worn. Stretch bolts on camshaft sprockets and covers are replaced rather than reused, the timing is verified by hand through two full turns, and adaptations are reset on the scan tool.

Two things get talked through before you commit. The first is access: some engines are a day's work from the front of the car, others need the engine or the gearbox out because the chain sits at the flywheel end, and that difference is most of the price. The second is whether the car is worth it, which you will get a straight answer on rather than a sales pitch. If we go ahead, the engine leaves with fresh oil to the correct approval and a new filter, and we will tell you plainly what oil interval to keep to, because that is what wears the next chain.

The cars we see

The engines we see share a layout more than a badge. Front-mounted chains on transverse four-cylinder petrols are usually reachable with the engine in the car, though the inner wing normally has to come apart to get at them. Chains that sit at the flywheel end, between the block and the gearbox, mean the engine or the box comes out, which is why two outwardly similar cars can be days of labour apart. Diesels often carry a second chain for the oil or vacuum pump, and that gets inspected while everything is open.

Timing Chain Replacement: what’s involved

A timing chain does the same job as a cambelt, keeping the crankshaft and camshafts in exact step, but it is a metal chain running inside the engine in oil. It is guided by plastic-faced rails and kept tight by a tensioner, usually fed by engine oil pressure. Chains have no fixed replacement interval and are meant to last a long time, but that depends on clean oil at the right level.

Over time the chain's pins and bushes wear, which lengthens the chain and is known as stretch. The guides wear and the tensioner can no longer take up the slack. The cam timing drifts, which the engine management picks up, and eventually the chain can jump a tooth or break. On most engines that means pistons hitting valves. Caught early, a chain replacement is a planned repair rather than an engine rebuild.

Signs of a worn timing chain

Rattle on cold start

A metallic rattle for a few seconds after a cold start, before oil pressure builds in the tensioner, is the classic early sign. If it lasts longer or gets louder, the chain or tensioner is worn.

Cam and crank correlation fault codes

Codes such as P0016 to P0019 mean the engine computer sees the camshaft and crankshaft positions drifting apart, which is typical of a stretched chain.

Engine management light

The light may come on with timing, camshaft sensor or variable valve timing codes, sometimes before any noise is obvious.

Rattle or whine when warm

A persistent rattle at idle or a whine that rises with revs when the engine is warm suggests a worn tensioner, broken guide or a very stretched chain.

Rough running or loss of power

Incorrect cam timing can cause misfires, hesitation, poor starting and reduced power.

Metal or plastic fragments in the oil

Pieces of broken guide can turn up at an oil change, and debris can block the oil pick-up.

Known engine with high mileage

Some engines have a history of chain problems, and at higher mileages we recommend checking chain wear even without symptoms.

When should a timing chain be replaced?

Unlike a cambelt, a timing chain has no scheduled replacement interval. It is replaced when there is evidence of wear: noise, fault codes, or measured timing deviation outside the manufacturer's limits. How long a chain lasts depends heavily on oil. Long service intervals, the wrong specification of oil or running with the level low all speed up wear. Some engines are known for chain problems, so on those we check chain wear at higher mileage or when buying a used car, not just when the rattle starts.

  • No fixed interval; replace when wear is confirmed
  • Check immediately if you hear a rattle on cold start that lasts more than a few seconds
  • Investigate any cam and crank correlation fault codes
  • Consider a check at higher mileage on engines known for chain wear
  • Short oil change intervals with the correct oil help chains last

If it’s left

A stretching chain rarely fixes itself. As the tensioner runs out of travel, the chain can slap and break guides, and eventually jump a tooth or snap. On most engines that means the pistons hitting the valves, and the repair becomes a cylinder head rebuild or a replacement engine instead of a chain kit.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about timing chain replacement

Fitting a tensioner and hoping

A new tensioner takes up slack for a while and quietens the rattle, which is why it gets tried. It does nothing about worn guides or a chain that has already lengthened, and the noise returns.

Extended oil intervals are harmless

The chain is lubricated and tensioned by engine oil. Long intervals, the wrong specification, or a level left low for months do far more damage than mileage alone.

Throwing sensors at a timing code

Correlation codes send people through camshaft sensors, solenoids and adjusters. Those parts do fail, but the code is describing a timing relationship, and it is worth measuring that before buying anything.

The noise stopped, so it is fine

A hydraulic tensioner pumps up as soon as oil pressure arrives and the rattle disappears within seconds. That is the tensioner doing its job on a worn chain, not the chain recovering.

Timing Chain Replacement: your questions answered

Can you keep me on the road while the car is in?

Bring it up at the point of booking. Courtesy cars depend on what has come back that week, and we also hire vehicles out by the day from the same site, which suits a job that runs across more than one day. If the car is still driveable we can collect it locally too.

Is the quote fixed, or can it change once you are inside the engine?

The figure covers the chain kit and the labour for the work described, and that does not move. What can change is what we find: a scored sprocket, a worn oil pump chain or a damaged adjuster. Those are priced and put to you for a decision before they go anywhere near the engine.

What does the guarantee cover on a job this size?

A year on the way the job was assembled, with the chain kit backed separately by whoever manufactured it. What nobody can guarantee is the rest of an engine that has been run on a worn chain, so if we see signs of wear elsewhere, such as low oil pressure or bearing noise, you will hear about it before you spend the money.

I am buying a used car with a known chain engine. Can you check it first?

Yes, and it is worth doing before you hand the money over rather than after. We listen from cold, scan for stored timing codes and read the deviation values where the engine supports it, which tells you far more than a test drive around the block. Bring it to us or arrange for the seller to meet you here.

Aren't timing chains supposed to last the life of the engine?

Many do, but it relies on regular oil changes with the right oil. Chains wear gradually, and some engine designs are known to wear chains or tensioners early. There is no service interval, so replacement is based on symptoms and measurements.

Is the Peugeot and Citroën 1.2 PureTech a timing chain engine?

No. The three-cylinder 1.2 PureTech uses a timing belt that runs in oil, known as a wet belt. It has a replacement interval and its own common faults, so it is handled as a cambelt job rather than a chain replacement.

Which engines are known for timing chain problems?

Well-known examples include BMW's N47 diesel, early VW Group 1.2 and 1.4 TSI petrol engines, early 1.8 and 2.0 TFSI engines, and the 1.6 turbo petrol used by BMW MINI, Peugeot and Citroën. Plenty of these run fine, so we diagnose each car rather than assume.

Can I keep driving with a timing chain rattle?

A brief rattle on cold start can go on for a while, but it tells you wear has begun. A rattle that lasts longer, a warning light or timing fault codes mean you should book in soon. The cost of waiting can be the engine.

What is the BMW N47 problem?

The N47 diesel has its timing chain at the back of the engine, next to the gearbox. Chain and guide wear there causes a rattle, often heard from the rear of the engine, and fixing it usually means removing the engine. We quote these as a complete job with all chains, guides and tensioners.
