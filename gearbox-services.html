Gearbox faults get misdiagnosed more often than almost anything else on a car, usually because the part that feels wrong is not the part that is wrong. A judder can be an engine mount. A whine can be a wheel bearing. A slipping automatic can be a fluid level that was never set at the right temperature in the first place. In the workshop

How we handle gearbox service at Hackbridge

The road test comes first, and where we can you come along for it. Being able to point at the moment it happens is worth more than any description, and some faults only appear on a cold gearbox at the first roundabout or after twenty minutes at motorway temperature. We log live data while driving: fluid temperature, clutch application values on a dual-clutch unit, slip across the torque converter lock-up, gear commanded against gear achieved. That is what tells us whether we are looking at the gearbox itself, the mounts and linkage around it, or a clutch.

Fluid is treated as evidence before it is treated as a consumable. Where the gearbox has a removable pan we take it off, so the magnets and the bottom of the sump can be seen: a fine grey fur is ordinary wear, while flakes, glitter or friction material is a different conversation entirely, and you get to look at it before we go any further. Level setting then follows the manufacturer's own procedure, at the specified fluid temperature with the vehicle sitting level, because a gearbox filled by eye either runs short or aerates the fluid.

We do not sell additives as repairs and we will not pressure flush a gearbox that is already shedding debris. There is no bottle that rebuilds a clutch pack. Where the fault is internal, the quote sets a rebuild against a reconditioned or used unit of the same gearbox code, with the supplier's warranty terms written down beside our own, so you are comparing real numbers rather than a hunch. If a unit has to come out, we price the clutch, rear main seal and mounts at the same time, because that labour is already being paid for once. Adaptation values are reset and recorded on the invoice.

The cars we see

Dual-clutch gearboxes bring in the most work, chiefly VW group DSG and S tronic, with the wet-clutch units in for scheduled fluid services and the dry-clutch ones more often in for judder and mechatronic faults. ZF eight-speed automatics in BMW and Jaguar Land Rover models respond well to a fluid and pan service at higher mileage. CVTs in Japanese models are a different animal again, needing their own specified fluid and a gentler diagnostic approach. Manual work is mostly cable linkage, mounts and tired synchromesh in high-mileage hatchbacks and vans.

Gearbox Services: what’s involved

Your gearbox uses sets of gears to match engine speed to road speed. A manual gearbox uses synchromesh rings to match gear speeds as you change, and runs in gear oil. An automatic uses a torque converter and hydraulic pressure, controlled electronically, to change gear, so the fluid is doing a hydraulic job as well as lubricating. Dual-clutch gearboxes such as VW's DSG combine two automated clutches with a mechatronic control unit.

Gearbox oil and automatic fluid break down with heat and time, and many gearboxes described as sealed for life will shift better and last longer with a fluid change at higher mileage. Most gearbox faults start small: a whine, a notchy change, a slow shift or a small leak. Diagnosing them early can often mean a fluid change, seal or linkage repair rather than a rebuilt gearbox.

Signs your gearbox needs attention

Whining or humming

A whine that changes with road speed or in certain gears often points to worn gear or bearing surfaces, or low oil.

Crunching into gear

In a manual, grinding when changing, especially into second or third, usually means worn synchromesh. It can also be a clutch that is not releasing fully.

Jumping out of gear

A gear that pops out under load or on the overrun suggests worn selector components, detents or gearbox mounts.

Harsh, slow or slipping automatic shifts

Thumping into gear, delayed engagement into drive or reverse, or revs flaring between gears are common signs of worn fluid, low level or a valve body or solenoid fault.

Judder from a DSG at low speed

Shuddering when pulling away or creeping in traffic is a common dual-clutch complaint, caused by clutch wear, adaptation problems or mechatronic faults.

Gearbox warning light or limp mode

If the car sticks in one gear and shows a transmission warning, the control unit has detected a fault and is protecting the gearbox.

Oil leaks

Drips from the driveshaft seals, input seal or gearbox casing lower the oil level. Automatic fluid is often red or brown; manual gear oil has a distinctive strong smell.

Stiff or vague gear lever

A lever that is hard to move or feels loose is often worn linkage bushes or stretched gear cables rather than the gearbox itself.

When should a gearbox be serviced?

Service requirements vary more with gearboxes than with almost any other part of the car. Some manufacturers specify regular fluid changes, particularly on wet-clutch dual-clutch gearboxes, while others describe the fluid as lifetime fill. In practice, fluid that has done a very high mileage has lost much of its protection, and a planned change can help an older automatic shift smoothly. Any change in shift quality, new noise or a leak is a reason to have it checked rather than waiting for a service.

  • Wet-clutch DSG units such as the six-speed DQ250: oil and filter change at about 60,000 km (roughly 37,000 miles)
  • Conventional automatics: many specialists recommend a fluid change around 40,000–60,000 miles, even if listed as lifetime fill
  • Manual gearboxes: oil change at high mileage or when changes become notchy
  • Check immediately after any leak, harsh shift, slip or warning light
  • Check fluid level after any driveshaft or seal work

If it’s left

Low or worn fluid increases heat and wear inside the gearbox. Small problems such as a leaking seal or slightly harsh shift can progress to worn clutches in an automatic, damaged bearings or broken synchromesh. At that point the choice is often a rebuild or replacement gearbox, which costs far more than early servicing or a seal.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about gearbox service

Pouring in universal ATF

A bottle claiming to suit forty specifications suits none of them properly. Friction modifiers differ between fluids, and the wrong one turns up as shudder, flare or harsh engagement within weeks of the change.

Topping up a gearbox with no dipstick

Many modern automatics are filled through a level plug with the fluid at a set temperature. Guessing through a filler hole almost always means overfilling, which whips air into the fluid and cooks it.

Buying a used gearbox on the model name

Gearboxes are identified by a code, not by the car they came out of. The wrong code can mean different ratios, a different final drive, or a control unit that will not talk to your car at all.

Restarting the car to clear limp mode

Switching it off and on again clears the symptom and loses the stored data that would have explained what happened. Leave the fault in memory and let us read it before it is wiped.

Gearbox Services: your questions answered

What does diagnosis cost, and what do I get for it?

We agree the diagnostic time with you before we start and show it separately on the quote, so you are never paying for open-ended investigation. What you get is the road test, a full transmission scan with live data, a fluid inspection and a written answer on whether this is a service, a repair or a replacement. That answer is yours whether or not you have the work done here.

If I need a replacement gearbox, what is guaranteed?

Our fitting carries the 12-month workmanship warranty. The unit itself is covered by whoever supplied it, and reconditioned and used gearboxes carry quite different terms, which we put in front of you before you choose between them. We also explain what the supplier expects of you, such as using the correct fluid and having the level set properly.

How long will the car be off the road?

A fluid and filter service is usually a same-day job. Diagnosis is often same-day too, unless the fault only shows when the gearbox is thoroughly hot and we need the car for longer. A gearbox coming out is generally one to three days, and we give you a date when we quote. Collection and delivery is available locally, and a courtesy car is subject to availability.

Is a fluid service worth doing on a car I am about to sell?

Often not, and we will say so. If the gearbox is shifting well, it is money that improves the car for the next owner rather than the price you achieve. If it is already shifting badly, fresh fluid will not repair worn clutches and we would rather tell you than take the work. On a car you are keeping for years, the calculation is completely different.

My automatic is sealed for life. Does it really need a fluid change?

The manufacturer may not list one, but fluid still breaks down with heat and use. Many transmission specialists recommend changing it around 40,000–60,000 miles. On a very high-mileage gearbox that has never had a change, we discuss the pros and cons before going ahead.

Does a DSG gearbox need servicing?

Wet-clutch DSG units such as the six-speed DQ250 have a scheduled oil and filter change, typically at about 60,000 km (roughly 37,000 miles). The seven-speed dry-clutch DQ200 does not have a routine oil change interval, but it can still develop clutch and mechatronic faults that we can diagnose.

Can you repair my gearbox or does it need replacing?

Many faults are outside the gearbox: linkages, mounts, seals, sensors or fluid. We rule those out first. If there is internal damage, we compare the cost of a rebuild with a reconditioned or used gearbox and explain both options.

Why does one fluid change not replace all the fluid in an automatic?

A lot of fluid stays in the torque converter and valve body when the gearbox is drained. A single drain and refill replaces only part of it, so on some gearboxes we recommend a second change after a short interval.

Is the noise from my gearbox or my clutch?

A noise that changes when you press the clutch pedal often comes from the release bearing, while a whine that changes with road speed in certain gears is usually inside the gearbox. A road test with a technician is the best way to tell.
