Purley Way hours on the clock that the odometer misses

The A23 Purley Way opened in 1925 as the Croydon bypass and is now lined with retail parks, so the stretch between Fiveways and the Lombard roundabout crawls through most evenings and all weekend. Drivers working between Airport House and Valley Retail Park spend a large part of the day at walking pace with the engine turning. That running time is what degrades oil, and none of it shows on the odometer.

The engines that suffer most are the ones with something fussy to feed. Hydraulic tappets, variable valve timing and turbo bearings all rely on oil that still has film strength and a working additive package, and both are gone long before the oil looks dirty. For cars used this hard, two or three interims between annual services is the normal pattern rather than an upsell.

The other half of the job is the inspection. Standing in queues on Wellesley Road and the approaches to the flyover means constant light braking, which polishes pads and takes the life out of them steadily. With the car on the lift we record pad and disc condition and measure tread across all four tyres, so you can see what six months of town work has actually cost. Tyres suffer in a less obvious way: tight turns in and out of car parks and service roads scuff the shoulders, and an edge that starts wearing unevenly carries on doing it for the rest of the tyre's life.

Everything we find is quoted in writing and nothing is fitted without a call, and repairs are covered for 3 months on our workmanship. Servicing

Interim Service: what’s involved

Service intervals have stretched a long way over the last twenty years, partly because oils and engines genuinely improved and partly because the test conditions behind those intervals assume steady, warm running. Not many cars in South London get that. A car that does six miles at a time in traffic never gets the oil hot enough to drive off condensation and fuel, and a car covering 20,000 miles a year reaches the mileage half of its schedule long before the date comes round.

The interim exists for both of those cases. It renews the engine oil and oil filter and puts the car on a lift for a proper look at the brakes, tyres, steering, suspension and fluids, so wear is found while it is still cheap to deal with. What it does not do is stand in for the annual service: the air filter, cabin filter and the longer-interval items belong to the full and major schedules.

When an interim service makes sense

You cover more than 12,000 miles a year

You will hit the mileage half of your schedule months before the annual date arrives. An interim keeps the oil fresh and gets the running gear looked at in between.

Almost all your journeys are short

School runs, station trips and local errands rarely bring the engine to full temperature, so fuel and moisture collect in the sump and the oil ages on time rather than on distance.

Six months have passed since the last service

The interim is a calendar job as much as a mileage one. Half a year is the point at which fresh oil and a set of eyes on the car start to be worth having.

You tow or carry heavy loads

A caravan, trailer or a van loaded to its limit puts sustained heat through the engine oil and the brakes, and both are worth checking more often than the standard schedule assumes.

Stop-start traffic every day

Queuing wears brakes far faster than open-road driving and keeps engine revs low while the engine idles hot, which is a harder life for oil than the mileage on the clock suggests.

The car works for a living

Private hire, courier and multi-drop use racks up engine hours as well as miles. Where a car is earning, the six-month check keeps unplanned days off the road to a minimum.

A diesel doing mainly urban miles

Particulate filter regenerations inject extra fuel late in the cycle, and some of it washes past the bores into the sump. Where journeys are too short to finish a regeneration, dilution builds up and the oil needs replacing sooner.

A noise or niggle you want looked at

A knock over speed bumps or a squeal on braking is worth mentioning when you book. The lift inspection is the part of the interim that finds the cause.

When should it be done?

An interim sits at the halfway point of your car's annual schedule, which for most cars means every 6 months or 6,000 miles, whichever arrives first. If you cover well over 12,000 miles a year, work from the mileage and you may fit two or three interims between annual services. If you cover very little but nearly always drive short distances from cold, work from the date instead. Cars used for towing, heavy loads or long spells idling are worth booking nearer 5,000 miles.

  • Every 6 months or 6,000 miles, whichever comes first
  • High-mileage drivers: work to the mileage, which can mean two or three interims a year
  • Short-trip and town use: at six months even when the mileage is low
  • Towing, roof loads or long periods idling: nearer 5,000 miles
  • Always in addition to the annual full service, never instead of it

How we carry out an interim service

  1. Check-in and history checkWe look at what was done at the last service and when, so the interim adds to it rather than repeating it, and we note any noise, warning light or concern you want us to pay attention to.
  2. Engine oil and oil filterThe oil is drained warm through the sump plug, a new filter and sump washer go on, and the engine is refilled with Castrol oil carrying your manufacturer's approval.
  3. Brakes and running gear on the liftWith the car raised we assess pad material and disc condition through the wheels, taking a wheel off where anything looks marginal, and inspect flexible hoses, pipes, driveshaft gaiters, ball joints, track rod ends and the exhaust with its mountings.
  4. Leaks and underbodyWe look for oil, coolant, fuel and brake fluid leaks, check the sump and filter housing for weeping, and note corrosion on brake pipes, subframes and sills before it becomes structural.
  5. Tyres and wheelsTread is measured across the width of each tyre rather than just in the middle, so uneven wear pointing to alignment or worn suspension is picked up. Pressures are set and wheels refitted to the correct torque.
  6. Fluids, lights and batteryCoolant strength, brake fluid, screenwash and power steering fluid are checked and topped up where needed, every light, wiper and the horn is tested, and the battery and charging output are put on test.
  7. Report, stamp and resetWe write up what we found, reset the oil service counter where the car has one, and stamp the book or update the record specifically as an interim service so the history stays accurate.
  8. HandoverWe go through the report with you, separate what needs doing now from what can wait until the annual service, and put anything extra in writing before it is booked.

Oil and parts we use

An interim uses the same oil we would put in at any other level: Castrol, to the approval your engine is built for, not simply the right viscosity. Oil filters are OE or OE-equivalent from suppliers such as MANN, Mahle and Bosch, fitted with the seals supplied and a new sump washer. If the inspection shows the air or cabin filter has given up early, we quote for it rather than fitting it and adding it to the bill.

  • Castrol engine oil to your manufacturer's approval
  • Oil filter of OE or matching quality, fitted with the seals supplied in the kit
  • New sump plug washer, or a replacement drain bolt where specified
  • Top-up fluids: coolant of the correct type, screenwash and brake fluid
  • Bulbs and wiper blades quoted separately where the check shows they are needed

If it’s left

Skipping an interim is not an emergency on a low-mileage car that mostly does longer runs. On a car doing 20,000 miles a year, or one that never properly warms through, it means the oil spends months past its useful life and the brakes and tyres go a full three months without anyone measuring them. Most of what an interim finds is cheap to put right at the time and expensive to put right late.

Interim Service in Croydon: your questions answered

I drive for a delivery platform around Purley Way. How many interims a year is sensible?

Work to the mileage and you will probably want two or three. At 20,000 miles a year you reach 6,000 roughly every ten weeks, and a lot of those miles are at low speed with the engine running. We will look at the oil at each visit and tell you if your interval should be shorter still.

I work evenings. How late can I drop the car off?

We are open Monday to Friday 8am to 8pm and on Saturday 8am to 5pm, so an early drop or a late collection is usually workable either side of a shift. An interim itself only takes an hour to an hour and a half. Booking ahead gets you the slot you want rather than the one that is left.

The car has done 18,000 miles since its last full service. What does it need now?

It is overdue a full service rather than an interim, because the air filter, cabin filter and brake fluid test all belong to the annual job. We would book the full service and then set your interims from that date. Bring whatever history you have and we will work out where the car actually sits.

Can an interim service replace my annual service?

No. The annual service covers the air and cabin filters and a wider inspection, and it is the one that keeps the car on the manufacturer's schedule. Two interims in a year are not equivalent to one full service. Treat the interim as extra protection in between rather than a cheaper alternative.

Does an interim service go in the service history?

Yes. We stamp the book or update the record and describe it accurately as an interim, with the date and mileage. That matters when you sell: a buyer or a dealer can see exactly what was done and when, and an interim written up as a full service helps nobody.

Does it include the air filter?

No, the air filter belongs to the full service. We do look at it, and if it is dark, damp or packed with debris we will say so and quote for it. Cars parked under trees or driven daily past dusty roadworks often need one before the annual service comes round.

My car says it is not due for another 18 months. Should I still book one?

Long-life schedules are calculated from how the car is actually driven, but they still assume the engine regularly reaches full temperature. If your driving is mostly short and urban, the calculation flatters the oil. An interim between the car's own service points is a sensible hedge and does not interfere with the manufacturer's schedule.

Will resetting the service light after an interim confuse the schedule?

Most cars keep an oil service counter separately from inspection or long-life items, so resetting the oil counter after an oil change is precisely what it is there for. Where a car uses a single combined counter we set it so the next full service still falls due on time, and we note what we did on your report.
