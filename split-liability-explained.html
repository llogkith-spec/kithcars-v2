Split Liability: Both Partly at Fault

Plenty of accidents are not one person’s fault. When insurers agree a split, everything downstream splits with it — your excess recovery, your no-claims, and what gets paid.

What a split actually means

Liability is apportioned as a percentage between the drivers. The usual outcomes are 50/50, 75/25 and 25/75, and each insurer pays the other’s losses in proportion.

SplitWhat it means for you at 50/50
Your repairHalf recovered from them
Your excessHalf returned to you
Your no-claimsUsually partly affected
Their repairHalf paid by your insurer

A 50/50 is not "nobody was at fault". It is "both were, equally", and it is recorded as a claim against you as well as against them.

When splits happen

  • Both vehicles moving and both contributed — lane changes, roundabouts, junctions.
  • No independent witness and two credible but incompatible accounts.
  • One driver clearly at fault but the other was speeding, had a light out, or could have avoided it.
  • Car parks, where right of way is often unmarked and ambiguous.
  • Neither insurer wants the cost of litigating a modest claim.

That last one is worth naming. A 50/50 is sometimes a commercial settlement between two insurers rather than a finding about what happened, and it can be offered simply because arguing costs more than conceding.

Should you accept one?

  1. Ask what evidence they are relying on. If the answer is "two conflicting accounts", your own evidence may still change it.
  2. Produce what you have. Dashcam footage, witness details, photographs of the road layout and final positions.
  3. Say clearly that you do not accept it. In writing. Insurers can and do settle on your behalf, and silence is taken as agreement.
  4. Weigh the cost of fighting. On a small claim the difference between 50/50 and 100% in your favour may be less than the time and stress of contesting it.
  5. Complain if they conceded without asking you. Your policy lets them settle, but a decision made without considering evidence you offered is worth challenging.

The practical effects

Your excess comes back in proportion, so on a 50/50 expect half. Your no-claims is usually affected, because your insurer has paid out and only recovered part of it. And the claim is declarable like any other.

If you had a credit hire car, a split is where that can leave a balance — the recovery only covers the proportion attributed to the other driver.

Your questions answered

Can I refuse a 50/50?

You can refuse to agree, and you should say so in writing if you believe it is wrong. Your insurer can still settle under the terms of your policy, but a documented objection with evidence attached makes that harder and gives you grounds to complain.

Does a split count as a fault claim?

Effectively yes for most pricing purposes, which is why it is worth contesting if the evidence is on your side.

What if I have dashcam footage?

Provide it immediately, before any decision is made. It is the single most effective thing at moving a split, and it is much less useful after a settlement has been agreed.

Does the split affect my repair?

Not the work itself. Your car is repaired properly either way. It affects who ultimately pays and how much of your excess comes back.
