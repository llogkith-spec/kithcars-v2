The Repair Was Done Badly. Now What?

If your insurer arranged the repair, your insurer is answerable for it. That single fact is the one most people do not know, and it is the one that settles most of these complaints.

The short answer

  • If your insurer arranged it, your insurer answers for it.
  • The test is whether the car is back to its pre-accident condition.
  • An independent engineer’s report is what usually decides it.
  • The Ombudsman is free, and handles these routinely.

Who is actually responsible

This is the part that decides everything else, and it turns on who chose the garage.

Who arranged the repairWho answers for it

Your own choice, with no such warning givenPossibly still your insurer
Your own choice, but the insurer took control of the repairPossibly still your insurer

That is the Financial Ombudsman Service’s stated approach. The practical upshot: if the work came through your insurer, complain to your insurer. Do not let them point you at the bodyshop and step back.

What the repair is supposed to achieve

The test is not whether the repair looks acceptable. It is whether the car has been put back to the condition it was in before the damage.

If it has not, the Ombudsman may require the insurer to have it put right by their repairer, to pay for you to use a repairer of your own, or — where the car cannot be properly restored — to write it off and pay you its pre-accident market value.

The evidence that actually wins

These complaints turn on evidence, and one kind beats all the others.

  1. An independent engineer’s report. The Ombudsman describes independent expert evidence as what it often finds most persuasive. Yours will cost you, and it is frequently the thing that decides it.
  2. Contemporaneous evidence beats later evidence. In one upheld decision the insurer’s paint specialist report was written after the event to record findings nobody had documented at the time, and little weight was given to it.
  3. Photographs of the car before the damage. The condition it was in beforehand is half the test, and almost nobody has this. Old MOT photographs, sale adverts and service records all help.
  4. A written opinion from another bodyshop. Cheaper than an engineer and still useful. Ask for the cause, not just the fault.
  5. Dates. When the car went in, when it came back, when you first complained. Timelines decide arguments about whether a fault was present on return.

The "you should have spotted it sooner" argument

Insurers sometimes argue a paint fault would have been visible within days of collection, so a complaint months later must be about something new.

It is not automatically wrong — fresh stone chips and kerbing genuinely do cause bubbling, and one Ombudsman decision went against the customer for exactly that reason. But it depends on the timeline, and in another the argument failed because the final inspection had happened only six days after the car was painted and other paint on the same car needed redoing within two months.

If that argument is put to you, ask for the dates it relies on and check them against your own.

How to escalate

  1. Complain to the insurer in writing. Not the bodyshop, if the insurer arranged it. Set out the fault, the dates and what you want done.
  2. Ask for their final response. That is the formal document that opens the next door.
  3. Get your own evidence while you wait. An inspection, photographs in daylight, a second opinion.
  4. Take it to the Ombudsman. Free, and they routinely handle repair-quality complaints. Normally within six months of the final response.
  5. Keep driving it or not? If it is unsafe, stop. If it is cosmetic, carry on and photograph it as it changes.

Your questions answered

The bodyshop says it is within tolerance.

Ask to see it with them, outside, in daylight. "Within tolerance" is reasonable for a measured difference and not for something you can see from across the road. If the insurer arranged the repair, put the complaint to the insurer regardless of what the bodyshop says.

Can I just take it somewhere else and send the bill?

Not safely. Tell the insurer the repair has failed and give them the chance to put it right first. The Ombudsman can require them to pay for another repairer, but that normally follows a complaint rather than a bill arriving unannounced.

How long do I have to complain?

Raise it with the insurer as soon as you notice. For the Ombudsman, it is normally six months from the insurer’s final response, and generally six years from the event. Sooner is always stronger, because paint faults are easier to attribute early.

Does it cost anything to go to the Ombudsman?

Not to you. It is free for consumers. You may choose to pay for an engineer’s report, and that is usually the money worth spending.

What if the car cannot be properly repaired at all?

Then the Ombudsman may require the insurer to write it off and pay the pre-accident market value. That is the remedy where a car cannot be restored to its previous condition.
