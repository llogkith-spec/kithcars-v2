How we handle wheel bearing replacement at Hackbridge

The corner is confirmed before a price goes on paper. That means driving the car at the speed the noise appears and loading each side in turn, then getting it in the air to feel for roughness and rock the wheel with hands at twelve and six, then again at three and nine, because movement at three and nine is a steering joint rather than a bearing. Tyres get a proper look in the same check: cupped or feathered tread roars in a way that is very easy to blame on a bearing, and it is worth ruling out before anybody spends anything.

How it comes apart depends entirely on the design. A bolt-on hub unit is a straightforward change on the car. A bearing pressed into the carrier is a different proposition: we would far rather take the carrier off and press the new bearing in on the bench, with the correct adapters, than drive one in on the car, because hammering a bearing through its outer race damages it before the car has turned a mile. The hub face is cleaned back to bare metal while everything is open, since corrosion there alone is enough to make a new disc run out of true and judder under braking.

Afterwards it is checked rather than assumed. Wheel speed signals are compared on the scan tool at road speed, which shows immediately whether the encoder ring is reading properly and facing the right way. Brakes go back with the slides and pad abutments cleaned and greased, since the calliper had to come off anyway. We will not replace a bearing on a description over the phone, and we will not change a good side to make a pair. The quote is per corner, and if a fixing shears or a carrier has seized into the arm, you get a phone call with what it adds before we go further.

The cars we see

Rear bearings on a lot of Fords and Vauxhalls are a complete bolt-on unit, which is why a rear can be quicker and cheaper than a front on the very same car. Front bearings on many European cars press into the carrier and need the press, and some rear setups bring the whole hub carrier off with them. Private hire hybrids covering motorway mileage wear bearings out on schedule rather than through impacts, while town cars tend to kill them on potholes. Vans carry the most weight at the back and go there first.

Wheel Bearing Replacement: what’s involved

Each wheel turns on a sealed bearing packed with grease, which supports the car's full weight and every cornering and braking load. On many modern cars the bearing is part of a bolt-on hub unit, while others use a separate bearing pressed into the hub carrier. Most also carry the ABS sensor's magnetic encoder ring in the bearing seal, so the bearing is part of the braking system's electronics as well.

Bearings wear out when the seal lets in water and grit, or when they are damaged by pothole or kerb impacts. The first sign is usually a hum or drone that rises with speed and is easy to mistake for tyre noise. As wear increases, play develops in the wheel. Replacing a bearing early is a straightforward job; leaving it can damage the hub, affect the ABS and, in extreme cases, let the wheel move on the hub.

Signs of a worn wheel bearing

Hum or drone rising with speed

The classic sign is a low roar that gets louder as speed increases, often most noticeable from around 30 mph upwards.

Noise changes when weaving

A worn bearing gets louder when it is loaded. Turning right puts weight on the left wheels, so a noise that grows when turning right usually points to a left-hand bearing, and the other way round.

Grinding or rumbling

As wear advances, the hum becomes a grinding or rumbling that you may feel through the floor or steering.

Play in the wheel

Rocking the wheel at the top and bottom with the car raised reveals movement if the bearing is badly worn.

ABS or traction warning light

On bearings with a built-in encoder ring, a damaged seal or excessive play can upset the ABS signal and trigger a warning light.

Vibration through the steering

A worn front bearing can cause vibration or looseness in the steering, especially when cornering.

Uneven brake pad wear

Play in the bearing can knock the pads back and cause uneven wear or a longer pedal on one side.

When should a wheel bearing be replaced?

Wheel bearings do not have a service interval; they are replaced when they become noisy or develop play. Many last well over 100,000 miles, but a heavy pothole or kerb strike can damage one much sooner. Because bearing noise builds gradually and sounds like tyre roar, it is easy to get used to it. If you notice a new drone at speed, especially one that changes as you steer, have it checked so it can be replaced before it wears further.

  • Replace when noisy, when play is found or when an ABS fault is traced to the bearing
  • Check after a heavy pothole or kerb impact
  • Many bearings last 80,000–150,000 miles, but it varies with roads and loads
  • Replace in pairs only if both sides are worn; a good bearing does not need changing
  • Check before an MOT if you hear a drone at speed

How we replace a wheel bearing

  1. Check-in and road testWe drive the car at varying speeds and weave gently to load each side, narrowing down which bearing is making the noise.
  2. Lift and checkWith the car raised we check each wheel for play and spin it by hand to feel roughness. Where needed we use a chassis listening device to confirm the corner.
  3. Written quoteWe confirm whether your car uses a bolt-on hub unit or a pressed bearing and quote in writing.
  4. Strip the cornerThe wheel, brake calliper, disc and ABS sensor are removed, and the driveshaft is released on driven wheels.
  5. Replace the bearingHub units are unbolted and replaced. Pressed bearings are removed and fitted using a press, pushing only on the correct race so the new bearing is not damaged.
  6. Check the ABS encoderWhere the encoder ring is in the bearing seal, we make sure it faces the ABS sensor. Fitted the wrong way round, the ABS will not work correctly.
  7. Torque the hub nutThe hub nut is tightened to the manufacturer's torque, and replaced if it is a single-use nut.
  8. Road test and handoverWe refit the brakes, check for ABS fault codes, road test to confirm the noise has gone and explain what we found.

Wheel bearing parts we fit

We fit OE or OE-equivalent wheel bearings and hub units from bearing manufacturers such as SKF, FAG, SNR and Timken, which supply car makers directly. Quality really matters here: cheaper bearings are known to become noisy again quickly. Bearing kits usually come with a new hub nut and circlips where required, and we replace single-use fixings rather than reusing them.

If it’s left

A worn bearing gets steadily louder and looser. Heat from a failing bearing can damage the hub, driveshaft seal and ABS sensor, adding to the repair. Play affects braking and steering precision, and excessive play is an MOT failure. In rare, neglected cases a bearing can break up and let the wheel move on the hub.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about wheel bearing replacement

A bearing can be regreased

Modern bearings and hub units are sealed for life and cannot be repacked. Once water and grit are past the seal the races are already marked, and no amount of grease brings them back.

Tighter is better on the hub nut

The hub nut torque is a specification, not a suggestion, and on many designs it sets the preload on the bearing. Over-tighten it and the bearing is crushed, and it will be humming again within months.

Every quote is for the same job

A bolt-on hub unit and a pressed-in bearing are hours of labour apart on cars that look alike from the outside. Comparing prices only means something once you know which design your car uses.

Replace the ABS sensor first

Where the magnetic ring lives in the bearing seal, a failing bearing shows up as a wheel speed fault, and a new sensor changes nothing. Read the live wheel speeds before buying a part.

Wheel Bearing Replacement: your questions answered

Can you do it while I wait?

A bolt-on hub unit is comfortable to wait for. A pressed bearing, especially on a car that has seen a few London winters, is better left with us, because the carrier usually has to come off and go on the press. We will tell you which yours is when we look it up, and we collect and deliver locally if that suits you better.

Will it need a wheel alignment afterwards?

Usually not. Changing a bolt-on hub unit does not disturb the geometry at all. Where the hub carrier has to come off, or an arm is removed to get at the bearing, an alignment check afterwards is worth having, and we will tell you which applies to your car before you decide.

What happens if a bolt snaps or the bearing is seized in?

It is a real possibility on a car that has spent years on salted roads. Work stops and you get a call with what it will take, whether that is extracting a bolt, replacing a hub carrier or sourcing a part. Nothing extra is charged without you agreeing the figure first.

How long should a new bearing last?

Fitted correctly, with a decent part, it should give the sort of life the original one did. What cuts that short is impact damage, a pressure washer aimed straight at the seal, and cheap bearings, which is why we fit known makes and keep the invoice on file in case it is ever needed.

How do I know it is a wheel bearing and not my tyres?

Both can hum at speed. Tyre noise usually changes with the road surface and sounds much the same when turning, while bearing noise tends to get louder when you steer one way and quieter the other. A road test and lift check confirm it.

Should both wheel bearings on an axle be replaced?

Only if both are worn. Unlike brakes, bearings do not need replacing in pairs for balance. We check both sides and tell you if the other is starting to go.

Can a wheel bearing cause an ABS warning light?

Yes. Many bearings carry the magnetic ring the ABS sensor reads. A damaged seal, excessive play or a bearing fitted the wrong way round can cause ABS and traction control faults.

Is a clicking noise on full lock a wheel bearing?

Usually not. Rhythmic clicking when turning sharply under power is more often a worn outer CV joint on the driveshaft. We check both during diagnosis.

Is it safe to drive with a noisy wheel bearing?

A light hum is not an immediate danger, but it will get worse and can damage surrounding parts. If the noise is loud, grinding or you can feel play in the steering, book in soon and avoid long motorway journeys.
