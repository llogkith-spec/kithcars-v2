Speed humps, suspension mountings and rust on Morden's avenues

Speed humps don't cause rust, but they do find out where it is. Across the St Helier estate and along roads near Ravensbury, traffic calming means the suspension compresses hard many times a day. On a sound car that's fine. On an older one with corrosion around the rear spring seats, strut tops or subframe mounts, those repeated loads work on steel that's already thin, and cracks and creaks can follow. This is the area within the MOT's 30cm prescribed zone, so it's taken seriously at the test.

Cars in Morden often live out on the street and do a lot of short trips to Morden station, the shops around Crown House or Morden Leisure Centre. Short trips don't give the underside time to dry, and road salt from the winter lingers in the seams. Rear arches and the area around the rear suspension take most of the spray, which is why we see so much rust there. Estates and people carriers used for family life on the estate also carry a lot of weight at the back, which puts more load through the rear mountings.

When you bring the car in, we lift it and check the mountings first, along with the sills and floor. If a strut top or spring seat is rusting, we cut out the corroded steel, form a new section and weld it in, then seal and protect it. It's more involved than a sill patch, as the suspension may need to come off to reach it, and we'll explain that in the written quote. We also check the other side of the car, because if one spring seat has rusted, the other is often not far behind.

Heading down Bishopsford Road or taking the A239 past Morden Hall Park, the drive is short. If the MOT has flagged a dangerous defect near the suspension, don't drive it; we can arrange collection. Work is covered by our 12-month workmanship warranty.

Welding & Rust Repair: what’s involved

Most cars have a monocoque body, meaning the shell itself acts as the chassis. Sills, floor pans, crossmembers and the areas around suspension and seat belt mountings carry load, so rust there weakens the car. Corrosion usually starts where water and road salt collect: inside sills, behind arch liners, along seams and around jacking points, and it often works from the inside out.

The MOT treats corrosion seriously. Excessive corrosion in load-bearing structure is a failure, particularly within 30cm of prescribed areas such as suspension, steering and seat belt mounting points. A proper repair means cutting back to sound metal, fitting a new steel section and welding it in, then sealing and protecting it inside and out. Covering rust with filler or a plate over rotten metal doesn't fix it, and a tester can fail an inadequate repair on a structural area.

Signs of rust that needs welding

Bubbling paint on sills or arches

Rust forming underneath the paint. It's usually more extensive than the visible bubbles suggest.

Holes or flaking metal underneath

Floor pans, sills, jacking points and crossmembers that flake or can be pushed through with a probe.

MOT advisory or failure for corrosion

An advisory is a warning worth acting on; a failure for structural corrosion must be repaired before the car can pass.

Soft or crumbling jacking points

If the sill crushes or the jack sinks into it, the structure around it is weakened.

Rust around suspension mountings

Corrosion near spring seats, strut tops, subframe or trailing arm mounts is structural and needs prompt repair.

Water in the footwells

Can be caused by holes in the floor, although blocked drains and door seals are more common causes, so we check.

Rust on door bottoms and boot floor

Often cosmetic rather than structural, but cheapest to repair while the rusted area is still small.

When should it be done?

Deal with structural rust as soon as it's found, ideally before the MOT so you have time to plan. Corrosion advisories are worth acting on, because another winter of salted roads can turn an advisory into a failure needing bigger repairs. Cosmetic rust on door bottoms or arch edges isn't a safety issue, but it's cheapest to fix while it's still at the bubbling stage. Older and classic cars benefit from a regular underside inspection.

  • MOT advisory mentioning corrosion: before the next winter or MOT
  • MOT failure for corrosion: before retest, and before driving at all if the defect was rated dangerous
  • Bubbling paint on sills, arches or door bottoms: while the rust is still small
  • Buying an older or classic car: underside inspection before or soon after purchase

How we repair rust and welding

  1. Inspection on the rampWe examine the underside, probe suspect areas and read any MOT failure sheet, separating structural corrosion from cosmetic.
  2. Written quoteWe explain what's structural, what's cosmetic and what should be prioritised. Rust sometimes proves more extensive once cut into, and we'll contact you before doing anything extra.
  3. Access and protectionTrim, arch liners, seats or carpets are removed as needed, and fuel lines, brake pipes and wiring are protected or moved away from heat.
  4. Cut out corrosionRusted metal is cut back until we reach clean, sound steel.
  5. Fabricate or fit repair sectionsNew steel sections are formed in the workshop, or pre-formed repair panels are used where available and a good fit.
  6. Weld inThe new metal is MIG welded in, seam welded where the structure requires it, and the welds are dressed back.
  7. Seal and protectJoints are seam sealed and primed, the underside is coated with underbody protection and enclosed sections are treated with cavity wax.
  8. Refit, check and hand overEverything is refitted, pipes and wiring are checked, and we explain what was repaired and anything else worth watching.

About Morden

Morden is a suburban town at the southern end of the Northern line, and grew rapidly after Morden Underground station opened in 1926, with its population rising from about 1,300 in 1921 to over 12,000 by 1931. Much of the housing is 1920s and 1930s semis and terraces, including a large part of the London County Council's St Helier estate, laid out along wide avenues and cul-de-sacs. Questions

Welding & Rust Repair in Morden: your questions answered

My car creaks over speed humps. Could it be rust?

It can be. Worn bushes and drop links are more common causes, but on older cars corrosion around spring seats and mountings can also make noise. We check both on the ramp so the right thing gets fixed.

Why does welding near the suspension cost more than a sill patch?

To reach the mounting safely we often have to remove suspension parts, and brake pipes and wiring need protecting from heat. The welding itself also has to be to a structural standard. We'll set out everything involved in the written quote.

Can you collect my car from Morden if it failed on corrosion?

Yes. If the defect was rated dangerous, it shouldn't be driven, so collection makes sense. We can also bring it back once the repair is done.

Will my car pass the MOT after welding?

We repair the items on your failure sheet to a standard that meets the MOT inspection requirements, although the tester makes the final decision at the retest. If we find other corrosion nearby that's likely to cause a problem, we'll point it out before the retest.

Is it worth welding an older car?

It depends on how much rust there is and what the car is worth. A couple of sill repairs on an otherwise sound car are usually worthwhile. Widespread corrosion across floors, sills and mountings can cost more than the car is worth, and we'll give you an honest view.

Can you just patch over the rust?

Not on structural areas. Welding a plate over rotten metal traps moisture so the rust carries on underneath, and a tester can fail it as an inadequate repair. We cut out to sound metal and weld in new steel.

What's the difference between structural and cosmetic rust?

Structural rust affects load-bearing parts such as sills, floors, crossmembers, chassis legs and the areas around suspension and seat belt mountings. Cosmetic rust is on outer panels like door skins and bolt-on wings, which aren't load-bearing and usually only fail an MOT if they leave sharp edges.

How can I slow down rust?

Rinse the underside during winter to remove road salt, keep door and sill drain holes clear, and deal with stone chips early. On older cars, cavity wax in the sills and underbody protection make a real difference, and we can apply them.
