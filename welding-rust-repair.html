In the workshop

How we handle welding and rust repair at Hackbridge

Preparation takes considerably longer than the welding. The battery is disconnected, fuel lines, brake pipes and looms near the repair are moved or shielded, carpets and sound deadening are pulled well back from the underside of a floor repair, and an extinguisher stays with the car for the whole job, because the wadding under a carpet will smoulder for a long time before anybody smells it. Where a repair sits close to the tank, the tank comes out rather than being worked around.

The metal itself is straightforward as long as nothing is rushed. Corrosion is cut back until the grinder finds bright steel rather than brown, the new section is made to fit the hole instead of the hole being opened up to suit a panel, mating faces get weld-through primer, and the joint is made the way the factory made it: plug welds where the original was spot welded, and a continuous seam where the structure calls for one. Welds are dressed back just enough to sit flush, because grinding them flat looks tidy and takes the strength straight back out of the job.

Then it is protected inside and out, which is the part that decides whether you are back in five years. Rust does not get buried here: no filler across a structural area, no fresh underseal sprayed over corrosion to hide it, and no aluminium panel welded as though it were steel, so tell us if the wing or bonnet is alloy. Each area is quoted on its own, so the sills can be done now and the boot floor next year, and if you arrive with an MOT failure sheet we work through it line by line and put a price against every item.

The cars we see

Age decides this work far more than the badge does. Cars past ten or twelve winters arrive with sill ends, jacking points and rear arch lips gone, and the underside of anything that has lived on salted South London roads is always worse towards the back, where spray collects. Vans rot along the sills and above the rear wheels, and ply lining hides it until it is bad. Older four-wheel drives with a steel chassis and aluminium panels corrode hardest where the two meet. Classics are welcome, and usually need the most honest conversation.

Welding & Rust Repair: what’s involved

Most cars have a monocoque body, meaning the shell itself acts as the chassis. Sills, floor pans, crossmembers and the areas around suspension and seat belt mountings carry load, so rust there weakens the car. Corrosion usually starts where water and road salt collect: inside sills, behind arch liners, along seams and around jacking points, and it often works from the inside out.

The MOT treats corrosion seriously. Excessive corrosion in load-bearing structure is a failure, particularly within 30cm of prescribed areas such as suspension, steering and seat belt mounting points. A proper repair means cutting back to sound metal, fitting a new steel section and welding it in, then sealing and protecting it inside and out. Covering rust with filler or a plate over rotten metal doesn't fix it, and a tester can fail an inadequate repair on a structural area.

Signs of rust that needs welding

Bubbling paint on sills or arches

Rust forming underneath the paint. It's usually more extensive than the visible bubbles suggest.

Holes or flaking metal underneath

Floor pans, sills, jacking points and crossmembers that flake or can be pushed through with a probe.

MOT advisory or failure for corrosion

An advisory is a warning worth acting on; a failure for structural corrosion must be repaired before the car can pass.

Soft or crumbling jacking points

If the sill crushes or the jack sinks into it, the structure around it is weakened.

Rust around suspension mountings

Corrosion near spring seats, strut tops, subframe or trailing arm mounts is structural and needs prompt repair.

Water in the footwells

Can be caused by holes in the floor, although blocked drains and door seals are more common causes, so we check.

Rust on door bottoms and boot floor

Often cosmetic rather than structural, but cheapest to repair while the rusted area is still small.

When should it be done?

Deal with structural rust as soon as it's found, ideally before the MOT so you have time to plan. Corrosion advisories are worth acting on, because another winter of salted roads can turn an advisory into a failure needing bigger repairs. Cosmetic rust on door bottoms or arch edges isn't a safety issue, but it's cheapest to fix while it's still at the bubbling stage. Older and classic cars benefit from a regular underside inspection.

  • MOT advisory mentioning corrosion: before the next winter or MOT
  • MOT failure for corrosion: before retest, and before driving at all if the defect was rated dangerous
  • Bubbling paint on sills, arches or door bottoms: while the rust is still small
  • Buying an older or classic car: underside inspection before or soon after purchase

How we repair rust and welding

  1. Inspection on the rampWe examine the underside, probe suspect areas and read any MOT failure sheet, separating structural corrosion from cosmetic.
  2. Written quoteWe explain what's structural, what's cosmetic and what should be prioritised. Rust sometimes proves more extensive once cut into, and we'll contact you before doing anything extra.
  3. Access and protectionTrim, arch liners, seats or carpets are removed as needed, and fuel lines, brake pipes and wiring are protected or moved away from heat.
  4. Cut out corrosionRusted metal is cut back until we reach clean, sound steel.
  5. Fabricate or fit repair sectionsNew steel sections are formed in the workshop, or pre-formed repair panels are used where available and a good fit.
  6. Weld inThe new metal is MIG welded in, seam welded where the structure requires it, and the welds are dressed back.

  8. Refit, check and hand overEverything is refitted, pipes and wiring are checked, and we explain what was repaired and anything else worth watching.

Steel and materials

Repairs are made with new steel of a suitable thickness for the area, either fabricated in the workshop or using pre-formed repair panels where they're available and fit well. After welding, joints are sealed, primed and protected with underbody coating, and enclosed sections such as sills are treated with cavity wax, which creeps into seams to slow future corrosion from the inside.

  • New steel sheet of the appropriate thickness
  • Pre-formed sill and arch repair panels where suitable
  • Weld-through primer and anti-corrosion primer
  • Seam sealer
  • Underbody protection and cavity wax

If it’s left

Rust doesn't stop on its own. It spreads, especially through winter when roads are salted. Structural corrosion reduces how well the body protects you in a crash and can weaken suspension and seat belt mountings. It will eventually fail an MOT, and the longer it's left, the more metal has to be replaced.

Worth knowing

What people get wrong about welding and rust repair

Underseal over rust protects it

Spraying a coating onto corroded metal seals the moisture in and hides what is happening underneath. On a car you are buying, fresh underseal is a reason to look harder rather than a selling point.

Sealant and foam will hold it

Filling a hole in a floor with sealant, expanding foam or a riveted plate keeps water in rather than out, and the surrounding metal goes in half the time. It also makes the eventual repair a bigger one.

Surface rust means structural rust

A light brown film on a subframe or a suspension arm is normal on any car that has seen a winter. What matters is metal that flakes in layers, sounds dull when tapped, or gives under a probe.

Leaving it until the MOT is due

Corrosion spreads fastest through a salted winter, and booking welding under retest pressure takes away your choice of when the car is off the road. It is the same work either way, done calmer and usually cheaper earlier.

Questions

Welding & Rust Repair: your questions answered

Do you paint the repair, or does it stay bare?

Underside work is sealed, primed and coated, which is the correct finish for that area and is not meant to be looked at. Where the repair is visible, such as an arch lip or a door bottom, it can be prepared and painted to match the car in the bodyshop, and that part is quoted separately from the welding.

Is the welding guaranteed?

Our workmanship carries a 12-month warranty, and that covers the areas we repaired. It cannot cover rust that starts somewhere else on a car that is corroding generally, so we always point out the areas we think are next, in writing, and you are not surprised by them a year later.

Can I keep driving it while I wait for a slot?

It depends what was found. Corrosion recorded as a dangerous defect means the car should not be driven at all, whether or not your old certificate is still in date. A major failure or an advisory is different: if your existing MOT has not expired, you can carry on while you wait for a date that suits you.

Do you take on older and classic cars?

Yes, and they are often the most satisfying work in the shop. Panel availability is what varies: some models have good reproduction sills and arches, others need sections fabricating here, and that changes the price more than the welding does. Send photographs of the worst areas and we will tell you which it is likely to be.

Will my car pass the MOT after welding?

We repair the items on your failure sheet to a standard that meets the MOT inspection requirements, although the tester makes the final decision at the retest. If we find other corrosion nearby that's likely to cause a problem, we'll point it out before the retest.

Is it worth welding an older car?

It depends on how much rust there is and what the car is worth. A couple of sill repairs on an otherwise sound car are usually worthwhile. Widespread corrosion across floors, sills and mountings can cost more than the car is worth, and we'll give you an honest view.

Can you just patch over the rust?

Not on structural areas. Welding a plate over rotten metal traps moisture so the rust carries on underneath, and a tester can fail it as an inadequate repair. We cut out to sound metal and weld in new steel.

What's the difference between structural and cosmetic rust?

Structural rust affects load-bearing parts such as sills, floors, crossmembers, chassis legs and the areas around suspension and seat belt mountings. Cosmetic rust is on outer panels like door skins and bolt-on wings, which aren't load-bearing and usually only fail an MOT if they leave sharp edges.

How can I slow down rust?

Rinse the underside during winter to remove road salt, keep door and sill drain holes clear, and deal with stone chips early. On older cars, cavity wax in the sills and underbody protection make a real difference, and we can apply them.
