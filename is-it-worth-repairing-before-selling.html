Repair It, or Sell It As It Is?

It depends on who is buying. A trade buyer prices damage at what it costs them to fix and deducts it either way. A private buyer prices it at what they imagine it costs, and that number is nearly always higher.

The short answer

  • Trade buyers price damage accurately. Private buyers price it two to three times too high.
  • Always fix: anything that suggests a crash, any rust, and kerbed alloys.
  • Leave: a single scratch or stone chips on an older car.
  • Recorded write-off? Documentation beats cosmetics.

That is the whole thing in one table. Against a dealer, repairing first mostly moves money from one pocket to another. Against a private buyer, it removes the thing they are mentally charging you three times over for.

The three worth doing every time

  • Anything that suggests a crash. A misaligned panel gap, a slightly different shade on one wing, a bumper sitting proud. Buyers do not think "scuff", they think "what else is wrong with it", and that discount is open-ended.
  • Rust, however small. It is the one kind of damage that gets visibly worse while the car sits on a forecourt, and it is the thing buyers inspect for.
  • Kerbed alloys, all round. Disproportionately visible, cheap to put right at £108 to £144 a wheel, and the first thing anyone notices walking up to a car.

The ones to leave

A single small scratch or stone chips on a car of some age. They read as normal use, and a car with no marks at all on a ten-year-old body sometimes raises more questions than it answers.

And anything where the repair costs more than the damage is taking off the price. That is a sum you can only do with a real figure, which is what the box below is for.

Get the order right

  1. Price the car both ways first. Look at advertised prices for the same model in good condition and in marked condition.
  2. Get the repair priced. A real figure, not a guess.
  3. Repair only where the gap is bigger than the cost. Including the three above, where the gap is usually invisible but real.
  4. Photograph it properly afterwards. In daylight, clean, from the angles that show the panels. Half the value of repairing is in the advert.

If the car has a recorded write-off

Different question, and the answer changes. A Category S or N is recorded permanently and the buyer will find it, so the discount is coming regardless of how the car looks.

What helps there is documentation rather than cosmetics: the repair invoice, the itemised estimate, photographs of the work. The biggest discount buyers apply to a recorded car is for the unknown, and a full file removes most of it.

Your questions answered

How much does a scuffed bumper take off the price?

Privately, usually far more than the repair costs, because the buyer is guessing. A bumper scuff is £290 to £390 with us; the mental deduction is often several times that.

Should I tell a buyer about a repair I had done?

If asked, yes, and having the invoice helps rather than hurts. A documented repair reads better than an undocumented one that a paint gauge finds anyway.

Is it worth a full respray before selling?

Almost never. The cost rarely comes back, and a freshly resprayed older car makes buyers wonder what was underneath.

What about a lease car I am handing back?

Different rules entirely — there are published thresholds in millimetres and anything under them is free. See the lease return page.

Can you tell me what is worth doing?

Bring it in and we will walk round it with you and say which marks a buyer will notice and which they will not. We would rather do two panels that matter than five that do not.
