Flood Damage: What Happens to the Car

Water in a car is not like other damage. It does not stop when it dries, it is rarely confined to what you can see, and it is the main reason a car is written off as Category A rather than repaired.

Why water is different

Most damage is where it looks. Water goes everywhere, sits in places that never dry, and keeps working for years — through wiring looms by capillary action, into connectors, inside sills and box sections, under carpet and sound deadening.

The corrosion starts immediately and continues long after the car looks and smells fine. An electrical fault eighteen months later, in a circuit nothing has touched, is the classic signature.

Fresh water against salt water

Fresh waterSalt water
CorrosionSlowerRapid and relentless
Electrical damageSeriousUsually terminal
Likely outcomeDepends on depth and durationAlmost always Category A or B

Salt water is effectively a death sentence for a modern car. It attacks connectors and circuit boards directly and it does not stop when the car dries out.

What to do immediately

  1. Do not try to start it. If water has reached the air intake, cranking it draws water into the cylinders. Water does not compress, and the engine destroys itself in a second.
  2. Disconnect the battery if it is safe to do so. Powered circuits sitting in water corrode far faster.
  3. Photograph the water line. Inside and out, before anything is moved or dried. It is the single most important piece of evidence.
  4. Note how long it was submerged. And whether the water was fresh or salt.
  5. Call your insurer. Flood damage is covered by comprehensive cover. Get it recovered rather than driven.

Why they get written off

The assessment is not about whether a flooded car can be made to work. It is about whether it can be made reliable and safe, and proving that is close to impossible once water has been through the electrics.

Insurers therefore write off flooded cars readily and categorise them cautiously, frequently as A or B. That is why flood-damaged cars disappear from the market rather than circulating repaired — or should.

Buying one is rarely a bargain

Flooded cars do appear for sale, often cleaned up and a long way from where they were flooded, sometimes after a flood event that made the news months earlier.

  • Damp or musty smell, particularly after the car has been shut up.
  • Silt or a tide line in the spare wheel well, under the carpet, or in the seat rails.
  • Mismatched or unusually clean carpet in an otherwise average car.
  • Corrosion on unpainted metal under the dash, on seat frames or on seatbelt bolts.
  • Electrical faults that come and go, or warning lights that clear themselves.

Always run a history check, and be especially careful with a car being sold far from where it is registered. A Category A or B car cannot legally return to the road at all.

Your questions answered

Is flood damage covered by insurance?

By comprehensive cover, yes, as accidental damage. Third-party-only cover does not cover damage to your own car.

My car was only in shallow water. Is it fine?

Possibly, and it depends on what the water reached rather than how deep it looked. The critical levels are the air intake, the ECU and the door-mounted modules, and those are lower on some cars than people assume.

Can a flooded car be repaired?

A lightly affected one, sometimes — carpets out, dried properly, affected components replaced. One that was submerged to dash height is not an economic repair and often not a safe one.

Can you assess mine?
