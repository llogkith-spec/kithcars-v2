Selling a Cat S or Cat N Car

You must disclose it, the buyer will find it anyway, and the price reflects it. What you can control is how much documentation goes with the car, and that is worth more than most sellers realise.

The short answer

  • You must disclose it, and a history check finds it anyway.
  • The biggest discount buyers apply is for the unknown, not the category.
  • Documentation is worth real money — invoices and strip-down photographs.
  • Put it near the top of the advert, with what the damage actually was.

What you have to disclose

A recorded write-off category is a material fact. Selling without disclosing it misrepresents the car, and a buyer who discovers it afterwards has a legitimate grievance and, potentially, a claim against you.

It is also pointless to hide. Any buyer running a history check — which costs a few pounds and takes a minute — sees it immediately. A seller who did not mention it has just told the buyer something about themselves rather than about the car.

What it costs you in value

Typical effect
Category NA noticeable reduction, proportionally harsher on cheaper cars
Category SMore than N, and more again on newer or desirable models
Old Category C or DSimilar to S and N respectively, sometimes worse because the marker tells a buyer less
Undocumented repairWorst of all — the discount buyers apply for the unknown

That bottom row is the one you can do something about. The difference between a recorded write-off with a full file of repair documentation and one with nothing is substantial, because buyers price uncertainty.

How to get the best price

  1. Gather every document. The repair invoice, the itemised estimate, photographs of the damage and of the repair in progress.
  2. Say it in the advert, near the top. Not buried at the bottom. A buyer who finds it late feels misled; one who reads it first is already considering it.
  3. Explain what the damage actually was. "Cat N — rear bumper and boot lid, no structural damage, repaired at [bodyshop] with invoices" reads completely differently from "Cat N".
  4. Offer an inspection. Welcoming an independent inspection is the strongest signal you can send.
  5. Price it realistically. Against other recorded cars, not against clean ones.

Who buys them

More people than sellers expect. Buyers who intend to keep a car long term care less about resale, and a well-documented Cat N at a fair discount is good value for someone doing high mileage or wanting more car than their budget otherwise buys.

Trade buyers and auctions are the quick route and pay the least. A private sale with full documentation takes longer and gets more.

Your questions answered

Do I have to tell a trade buyer?

Yes. The obligation to describe the car accurately does not depend on who is buying, and a trade buyer will check regardless.

Can the marker ever be removed?

Not if it was correctly applied. If it was recorded in error, that is a correction through the insurer who recorded it, and you will need evidence.

Does an MOT pass prove the repair was good?

No, and it is worth being clear about with buyers. An MOT is a roadworthiness check at a point in time; it does not assess the quality of a structural repair.

Will you provide documentation if you did the repair?

Yes. Itemised invoice, the estimate, and the strip-down photographs. That file is genuinely worth money when you come to sell.
